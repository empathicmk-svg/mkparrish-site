There is a sentence on your website that any of your competitors could lift, paste onto their own site, and run without a single objection from their legal team or their conscience. Probably there are many. I want to talk about the opposite kind of sentence — the one that, if a competitor tried to copy it, would make them look ridiculous, because everyone would know it wasn't theirs to say.

That sentence is your positioning. Everything else is decoration.

Most brand language fails a test it doesn't know it's taking. The test is not "is this true?" Almost all corporate copy is true. *We're committed to quality. We put customers first. We deliver results.* True, every word, and completely worthless, because the truth of it is shared by the entire market. A claim that everyone can truthfully make is not a position. It is the price of entry. You don't get points for it; you only lose points for omitting it.

> The question is not whether your claim is true. The question is whether it would still be available to your biggest competitor. If they could say it too, you haven't said anything — you've just paid the cover charge to be in the category.

The good sentence is *defensible*, which is a stronger and rarer property than *true*. A defensible claim is one that is true for you and would be a lie, or a stretch, or an embarrassment, for the people you compete with. It draws a line they cannot cross without looking like they're impersonating you. That line is the entire game. Everything a brand is trying to do — be chosen, be remembered, be worth a premium — depends on having at least one line a competitor can't stand on.

## How to Find the Sentence

You don't write your way to it. You decide your way to it, and then write it down. The decision has a shape, and you can pressure-test any candidate sentence against it.

Take whatever your homepage currently leads with and run it through these three filters in order:

1. **The competitor test.** Could your nearest competitor put this exact sentence on their site? If yes, delete it and keep digging. You're still in category language. (See: every "trusted by industry leaders" on the internet.)
2. **The wince test.** Does the sentence make you slightly nervous — because it's specific enough that someone could disagree, or narrow enough that it turns some people away? Good. The wince means you've said something with an edge. Claims with no edge have no hold.
3. **The proof test.** If a skeptic read it and said "prove it," could you? A defensible claim has receipts — a method, a track record, a refusal, a specific way of working. If you can't back it, it's not bold positioning, it's just a bigger version of the same vague promise.

A sentence that survives all three is rare and worth more than the rest of your homepage combined. It is the thing you build the brand on top of, the line you repeat until you're sick of it and then repeat a hundred more times, because the moment you're sick of it is roughly the moment the market is starting to learn it.

Here's the part founders resist: the defensible sentence almost always costs you something. It excludes a segment. It picks a fight with a popular idea. It commits you to a way of working you can't quietly abandon next quarter. That cost is not a bug. It is the proof that the sentence means something. Free claims — the ones that cost you nothing to make — are worth exactly what they cost. The sentence only you can write is the one you had to give something up to earn.

So go find yours. Not the truest thing you can say about your company. The truest thing you can say that no one else is allowed to.
