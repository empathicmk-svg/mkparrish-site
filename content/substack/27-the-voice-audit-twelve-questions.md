You can feel when copy is off-brand, but "it doesn't sound like us" is a useless note to act on, because it doesn't tell you *where* or *why.* The skill professionals have that most founders don't isn't a magic ear — it's a set of specific diagnostic questions they run, consciously or not, that locate the exact sentences where voice fails. This is that diagnostic, written down: twelve questions you can run on any piece of copy — a homepage, an email, a bio, a post — to find precisely where it goes generic and what to do about it. Print it. Run it on the thing you're about to publish.

A voice audit isn't about taste in the vague sense. It's about catching the specific, recurring ways real voice gets sanded into category mush — the hedges, the borrowed phrases, the abstractions, the throat-clearing. Each question targets one failure mode. Where a piece "fails" a question, you've found a concrete edit, not a vibe.

> "It doesn't sound like us" is a feeling. A voice audit turns the feeling into a list of specific sentences to fix. The difference between someone who can *sense* bad copy and someone who can *repair* it is a set of questions sharp enough to locate the problem.

Run the twelve questions in order on whatever you're working on. For each, the rule is the same: where the answer is "yes, this is happening," mark the sentence. The marked sentences are your edit list.

## The Twelve Questions

### Specificity (is it about anything real?)

1. **The competitor test.** Could a direct competitor publish this sentence unchanged? *If yes:* it's category language, not yours. Rewrite toward the specific claim only you can make. (This catches more bad copy than any other single question — run it on every line.)
2. **The abstraction test.** Does it use a vague benefit word ("streamline," "empower," "unlock," "optimize," "elevate") where a concrete thing belongs? *If yes:* replace the abstraction with the specific action or outcome it's hiding. "Streamline your workflow" → name the actual annoyance it removes.
3. **The example test.** Does it claim something without a single concrete example, number, or scene? *If yes:* the claim is floating. Anchor it to one specific, checkable thing. Specificity is the fastest exit from generic.
4. **The "so what" test.** For each feature or benefit, can a reader still ask "so what?" *If yes:* you stopped one level too high. Push to the thing they actually feel — the meeting they don't have, the hire they don't make, the night they sleep.

### Conviction (does it believe anything?)

5. **The hedge test.** Count the qualifiers — "can help," "may," "often," "tends to," "we believe," "it depends." *If many:* the copy is protecting itself instead of claiming something. Cut hedges; state the position. (See: "The Real Cost of 'It Depends.'")
6. **The wince test.** Is there a single sentence specific or bold enough to make you slightly nervous? *If none:* it's too safe to be memorable. Safe copy has no edge to catch on. Find one true thing worth the small risk of saying.
7. **The opponent test.** Is there any conventional wisdom this copy is pushing *against*? *If no:* you have description, not position. Name what you disagree with — an argument needs an opponent.
8. **The "just" test.** Search for self-shrinking words: "just," "kind of," "simply trying to," "a little." *If present:* you're apologizing for your own value. Delete them and read it back — it gets accurate, not arrogant.

### Voice (does it sound like a person?)

9. **The read-aloud test.** Read it out loud. Are there sentences you'd never actually say to a smart friend? *If yes:* those are the "professionalized" ones where the voice died. Rewrite them closer to how you'd really say it, then fix only the grammar.
10. **The throat-clearing test.** Does it open with a runway — "In today's fast-paced world," "We hope this finds you well," "As a company, we believe"? *If yes:* cut to the first sentence that actually says something. (See: "Nobody Reads the Second Paragraph.")
11. **The rhythm test.** Is every sentence the same length and shape? *If yes:* the monotony is a sign the voice has been flattened. Real voice varies — a long build, then a short punch. Break the metronome.
12. **The fingerprint test.** Is there one phrase, metaphor, or aside in here that's unmistakably *this person* — something a generic writer wouldn't have produced? *If no:* the load-bearing weirdness got sanded off. Put one back. The fingerprint is the whole point.

### How to use the results

Tally where the piece failed. The *pattern* of failures tells you the diagnosis:

- **Mostly Specificity fails (1–4)?** Your copy is vague, not wrong. You know what you think; you're saying it too abstractly. Fix: concrete examples and claims.
- **Mostly Conviction fails (5–8)?** Your copy is hedged, not vague. You're protecting yourself out of the consideration set. Fix: state positions, cut qualifiers.
- **Mostly Voice fails (9–12)?** Your copy is competent but dead — over-professionalized. Fix: write closer to speech, keep the fingerprints.

Most pieces fail clustered in one category, and that cluster *is* your recurring voice problem — the thing to watch for in everything you write, not just this piece. Run this audit on three things you've published recently and the pattern will be obvious. That pattern is more valuable than any single edit, because once you can name your own failure mode, you start catching it as you draft instead of after you publish.
