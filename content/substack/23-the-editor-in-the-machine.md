I've spent a few of these essays on what AI can't do for your writing — it can't supply a point of view, can't make a positioning decision, can't say the specific true thing only you would say. All of that holds. But I'd be doing you a disservice if I left it there, because the "AI is generic slop" take is just as lazy as the "AI will write everything" take, and both will cost you. The honest position is more useful and more boring: AI is a genuinely excellent *editor* and a genuinely poor *author,* and almost all the value is unlocked by knowing which seat to put it in.

Think about the two halves of writing. There's *authorship* — deciding what to say, which argument to make, what to include and refuse, the specific examples and the point of view. And there's *editing* — taking something that already says the right thing and making it tighter, clearer, better-structured, free of repetition and clutter. Authorship requires a stake in the outcome, a self, a position. Editing requires a sharp eye applied to existing material. AI has no stake and no self, which is why it's a bad author. But a sharp eye applied to existing material? That it can do, tirelessly, instantly, at a level that genuinely improves most people's drafts. The trick is to never let it author, and to lean on it hard as an editor.

> Used as an author, AI gives you a confident average you have to fight to make specific. Used as an editor, it makes your specific thing sharper without sanding off what made it yours. Same tool, opposite results — the entire difference is which job you hand it.

The failure mode I see is people using AI for exactly the wrong half. They ask it to *generate* — "write me a LinkedIn post about X" — and then spend their energy editing the genericness out of what it produced, fighting upstream against the average the whole way. That's backwards and exhausting. Flip it. *You* author — messily, in your own voice, with your own point of view and your real examples, even if it's rough. Then hand that draft to the machine and ask it to *edit.* Now you're working with the current instead of against it: your specificity is already in the material, and the tool is just polishing it. The post still sounds like you, because you wrote it. It's just cleaner.

## A Workflow That Keeps Your Voice

Here's a practical division of labor that uses AI for real leverage without letting it flatten you into everyone else.

1. **Author the raw draft yourself.** Get your actual thinking down — point of view, argument, the specific examples and opinions — in your own words, however rough. This is the irreplaceable part. Don't outsource it; it's the only part that was ever going to make the piece worth reading.
2. **Let the machine diagnose, not rewrite.** Instead of "rewrite this," ask "where does this drag? what's repetitive? where's the argument unclear? what did I over-explain?" Diagnosis keeps you in the author's seat — it points at problems and lets *you* fix them in your voice, rather than handing back a smoothed-over version that's lost your fingerprints.
3. **Use it for the mechanical passes.** Catching the word you've used four times, tightening a flabby transition, flagging a sentence that's structurally confusing, converting the finished essay into an email or a thread — this is genuine, real leverage, and none of it touches your point of view. Let it have all of this.
4. **Reject fluent drift.** When you do let it suggest line edits, watch for the moment a "cleaner" version is actually a blander one — where it's smoothed a sharp, slightly awkward sentence into something more generic. Fluency is the model's gravity. Your job is to keep the load-bearing weirdness even when the machine politely suggests removing it.

The meta-point is about leverage, properly understood. The promise of AI in writing was never "replace the writer." It's "remove the parts of writing that don't require a self, so the writer can spend more of their finite attention on the parts that do." Editing yourself is slow and a little soul-deadening; offloading the mechanical layer of it frees you to think harder about the thing that actually matters — what you're saying. Used that way, AI doesn't make your writing more generic. It gives you more hours to make it specific.

So put the machine in the editor's chair, not the author's. Write the thing yourself, in your own voice, because that part is yours and always will be. Then let the tireless eye make it sharper. That's not cheating, and it's not slop. It's just a very good editor who works for free and never gets tired of your draft — which, if you've ever tried to edit your own work at midnight, you know is worth a great deal.
