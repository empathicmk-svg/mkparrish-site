Here is an uncomfortable fact about the thing you spent all afternoon writing: most people will read the first sentence, maybe the second, and then decide whether to keep going. The decision is fast, mostly unconscious, and mostly *no.* They will not reach your beautiful third paragraph where the real argument finally arrives. They will not get to the part you're proudest of, the part you buried in the middle because you were "building up to it." There is no building up to it online. There is the first sentence, and then there is the back button.

This is not a reflection of your reader's intelligence or attention span. It's the physics of the medium. A person online is not reading; they're triaging. They have nineteen other tabs, a notification stack, and a default assumption — learned from a decade of disappointing links — that whatever they clicked probably isn't worth their time. Your writing isn't competing with other writing. It's competing with leaving. And "leaving" is winning by a wide margin at every moment, which means every sentence has exactly one job: earn the next one.

> Writing for the web isn't writing paragraphs. It's writing a series of reasons to keep reading, each one purchased by the sentence before it. The moment a sentence stops earning the next, the reader is gone — and they don't come back to give you a second chance at paragraph three.

The classic mistake is the windup. We're taught, in school and in books, to establish context, set the scene, build toward the point. That structure assumes a captive reader who has committed to finishing — a reader who exists for a novel and does not exist for your landing page, your email, or your post. Online, the windup is where you lose everyone. By the time you arrive at the point you were "setting up," the audience that needed the setup already left, and the audience that stayed didn't need it. The windup serves no one. It only delays.

## Writing Like They're About to Leave

Once you accept that the reader is always halfway out the door, the craft changes. You stop building toward your best material and start leading with it. A few things this looks like in practice:

1. **Put the best line first.** Whatever your strongest sentence is — the sharpest claim, the most surprising idea, the thing you were saving — move it to the top. Yes, the very top. The reward for reaching your best material should not be patience; nobody's that patient anymore. Lead with the thing that earns the read.
2. **Open with a hook, not a runway.** The first sentence's only job is to make the second one unavoidable. A concrete claim, a contrarian flag, a sharp question, a specific image — anything but throat-clearing. "In today's fast-paced world" is a runway. Burn it.
3. **Front-load every unit.** This isn't just about the opening; it's fractal. Every section, every paragraph, every email — the point goes first, the support goes after. Assume the reader bails at any line and make sure they got the value before they did. Write so that even the person who quits early leaves with the gist.
4. **Earn each sentence with the one before.** Read your draft and, at every line, ask: does the previous sentence make this one feel necessary? If there's a spot where attention could reasonably wander — a slow patch, a redundant beat, a windup — that's where you lose them. Cut it or sharpen it. The slow patches are leaks.

There's a deeper discipline underneath the tactics, and it's about respect. Writing like the reader is about to leave is not a cynical hack; it's the most respectful possible stance toward someone else's time. It says: *I know your attention is scarce and borrowed, so I'm going to give you the good part immediately and not make you dig for it.* The buried lede, by contrast, is a small act of disrespect — it makes the reader pay, in patience, for value you could have handed over for free.

So here's the test for anything you publish this week. Read only the first sentence, then stop. Would *you* keep going? Be honest. If the first sentence is a runway, a windup, or a throat-clear, you've already lost the reader you worked so hard to attract — at the one moment you had their full attention and spent it on setup.

Lead with the good part. There may not be a second paragraph, as far as the reader is concerned. Write like there isn't.
