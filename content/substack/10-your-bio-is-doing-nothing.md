Your bio is the most-read thing you've written and the least time you've spent. Every prospect reads it before the call. Every podcast host pulls from it. Every person deciding whether to take you seriously opens the LinkedIn About section first. It is doing more sales work than your homepage. And it almost certainly opens with a sentence like "an accomplished professional with over a decade of experience," which is a sequence of words that has never once made a single human being lean forward.

Here is what your bio is currently doing: nothing. It is occupying the space where persuasion should be. "Accomplished professional" tells the reader you are reluctant to claim anything specific. "Passionate about helping businesses grow" tells them you've read other bios. "Proven track record of delivering results" tells them you'd rather gesture at success than name one. Each phrase is a small act of hiding, and the reader — who is skimming, who is deciding fast — registers the hiding as a lack of confidence, which is the precise opposite of what a bio is for.

> A bio is not a résumé in paragraph form. A résumé lists what you've done. A bio makes an argument for why you're the person to do the next thing — and an argument can't be made out of adjectives.

The reason bios come out this way is that writing about yourself is genuinely hard, and the category dialect for "self" is even more clichéd than the dialect for companies. So people reach for the safe, accomplished-sounding phrases precisely because they're safe — they can't be challenged, because they don't claim anything. But "can't be challenged" and "can't be believed" turn out to be the same property. A claim with no edge gives the reader nothing to hold, and nothing to hold is nothing to trust.

## The Three Jobs of a Bio

A bio that works is doing three things at once, and you can audit yours against them in about ninety seconds.

1. **Establish what you do — specifically enough to exclude.** Not "marketing consultant." "I rewrite positioning for second-time founders whose first company taught them what generic copy costs." The first is a category. The second is a person with a point of view. Specificity is the whole job; it's what separates a bio from a job title.
2. **Earn the claim with one piece of proof, not five adjectives.** Replace "proven track record" with the actual track. One concrete, checkable thing — a number, a named outcome, a specific transformation — does more than a paragraph of "results-driven." Show one receipt and the reader extends you credit for the rest.
3. **Sound like a person who has a way of seeing things.** The best bios have a point of view baked in — you can tell what the person believes about their work, not just what they've done. That's the part no competitor can copy, because it's not a credential, it's a perspective.

The third one is the difference between a bio that informs and a bio that converts. Credentials inform. Perspective persuades. When a reader finishes your bio and knows not just *what you do* but *how you think about it* — and agrees, or is intrigued — they've already started trusting you. That's the entire purpose of the paragraph, and "accomplished professional" accomplishes none of it.

Try this, right now: open your bio and delete every adjective that could appear in a stranger's bio without changing its meaning. *Accomplished. Passionate. Proven. Dedicated. Results-driven. Seasoned.* Watch how much of your bio disappears, and notice that nothing of value left with it — because those words were never carrying value, only occupying the place where value should be. Now write back in, one sentence at a time, the specific things only you could honestly say. It will be shorter. It will be harder to write. It will, for the first time, be doing the job.

Your bio is the highest-leverage paragraph you own. Stop letting it hide.
