When people ask what I do, the easy answer is "I'm a copywriter," and I've mostly stopped giving it, because it's the kind of imprecise shorthand I spend my days helping clients escape. "Copywriter" makes it sound like you hand me a brief and I make the sentences prettier — that the value is in word choice, in polish, in a knack for a turn of phrase. That's not the work. The words are the last five percent. The other ninety-five is a thing that doesn't have a clean job title, and it's the part worth paying for: I help people decide what they're for, and then I make the page say it without flinching.

If you've read much of The Margins, you already know the through-line, because I keep arriving at it from different doors. The tagline isn't the problem. The prompt isn't the strategy. "It depends" is avoidance. The comprehensive homepage is a coward's homepage. Every one of those essays is the same argument wearing a different outfit: **clarity is a decision before it's a sentence, and most "writing problems" are decisions someone hasn't made yet.** You can't wordsmith your way to a position you haven't taken. The blank space where the good sentence should be isn't a copy gap. It's a choice you've been avoiding, and no writer — human or machine — can make it for you.

> I don't get hired to write. I get hired to help people decide — who they're for, what they're against, the one claim they'll defend — and then to write it down so plainly that it can't be misread. The writing is the easy part. The deciding is the work, and it's why this isn't copywriting.

So here's what actually happens when someone works with me, stripped of the job title. We start with the uncomfortable questions, not the page. Who is this really for — narrowly enough to turn someone away? What does the market believe that you think is wrong? What's the one thing you do that a competitor couldn't credibly claim? What are you willing to be known for, and what are you finally willing to stop trying to be? These are not copy questions. They're positioning questions, identity questions, business questions. And until they're answered, every sentence we'd write would just be a more polished version of the vagueness you already have. Most of my clients arrive thinking they have a writing problem. Almost none of them actually do.

## Why the Words Come Last

There's a reason I'm so insistent on the order, and it's not philosophical — it's practical. Words built on undecided positioning don't hold. You can pay for a beautiful homepage, love it for a month, and watch it stop sounding true the moment the business shifts, because it was never anchored to a decision, only to a mood. But words built on a real position are durable. They keep working. They give your whole team — sales, marketing, the happy customer telling a friend — the same clear thing to repeat. The clarity compounds. That's the difference between copy that decorates and copy that holds weight, and it lives entirely in whether the deciding happened first.

This is also why I'm not threatened by AI, and why I keep writing about it here. The models are very good at the last five percent — drafting, restructuring, polishing — and genuinely useless at the ninety-five, because the ninety-five is *judgment and decision,* and a model has neither a stake nor a self. AI made competent words free, which means competent words are now worth almost nothing, which means all the value rushed upstream to the exact place I already work: deciding what's worth saying. The flood of generic content didn't make my job obsolete. It made it the only job that mattered.

Here's how the work actually shows up, depending on where you are:

- **If you can't say what you do in a sentence** — the four-minute answer, the winding "it depends" — that's a positioning engagement. We find the decisions you've been straddling and make them, and the sentence falls out the other side. (That's **The Rewrite**, and it's where most people should start.)
- **If your homepage is a catalog instead of an argument** — competent, complete, and forgettable — that's a homepage rebuild: the hierarchy, the one defensible claim, the courage to cut. (That's **The Build**, or a focused homepage engagement.)
- **If your own story has gone generic** — the bio that hides, the LinkedIn that fogs, the origin story you sanded smooth — that's identity work as much as copy work, and it's some of my favorite. (That's **The Rewrite**, turned on you instead of your company.)
- **If you'd rather run the process yourself**, I've documented the actual frameworks — the three-layer positioning method, the voice audit, the playbooks — so you can. (That's the shop, and the paid side of The Margins.)

I want to be honest about the boundary, because precision is the whole brand: I'm not a fit for everyone. If your positioning is already sharp and you just need volume, hire a production shop — that's not me, and I'll tell you so on the first call. If you want someone to validate a vague idea with pretty words, I'm the wrong person; I'll push on the idea, and some people don't want that. The work only makes sense if you're willing to *decide* — to give something up, exclude someone, take a position you'll have to defend. That willingness is the actual prerequisite. The talent for sentences is mine to bring. The nerve to commit to a position has to be yours.

So that's what I actually do. Not "make your copy better." Help you decide what you're for, and then write it down so clearly that the right people recognize themselves in it and the wrong ones move on. It's why The Margins exists — to do that thinking in public, before the edit, where you can watch how it works. And it's why, if your words have never quite sounded as good as the thing they're describing, the fix probably isn't a better writer. It's a decision, finally made, and then said plainly.

That part, I can help with.
