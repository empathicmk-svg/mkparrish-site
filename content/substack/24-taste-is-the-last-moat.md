When the cost of producing something drops to zero, the thing that was scarce stops being the production and becomes the *judgment* — knowing which of the infinite things you could now make is actually worth making. We are living through exactly this shift in writing, design, and content, and most people are responding by celebrating the wrong half. They're thrilled that they can now produce ten times more. They should be paying attention to the fact that production was never the bottleneck, and now that it's gone, the real bottleneck is fully exposed: taste. The ability to tell good from generic, to choose, to know what's worth saying. That's the last moat, because it's the one thing the tools made *more* valuable instead of less.

Here's the logic. For most of history, the ability to produce competent content was itself a moat — it took skill, time, and money to write well, design well, make things well, so the people who could do it had an advantage simply by being able to. AI collapsed that. Competent production is now cheap and near-instant and available to everyone, which means it's worth almost nothing as a differentiator. When everyone can produce the competent average, the competent average is the new baseline, the table stakes, the thing that no longer distinguishes anyone. And the moment production commoditizes, all the value rushes upstream to the thing that *can't* be automated: the judgment about what to produce in the first place.

> When anyone can make anything, the scarce thing is knowing what's worth making. Production was the old moat and it's gone. Taste is the new one — and unlike production, it gets *more* valuable the cheaper making becomes.

Taste is a slippery word, so let me be concrete about what I mean, because it's not vibes or aesthetic snobbery. Taste is the capacity to make fine distinctions in quality and relevance — to look at ten competent options and know that nine are generic and one is alive. To feel the difference between writing that's clean and writing that *lands.* To know what to cut. To recognize the specific over the average. It's a trained discernment, and here's the crucial part: it's the one input AI fundamentally lacks. A model can generate a thousand options. It cannot tell you which one is good, because "good" requires a point of view about what good *is,* and the model has none. It can produce. It cannot judge. Judgment is the human's job now, and it's the whole job.

## Building the Moat

If taste is the advantage that doesn't commoditize, the strategic question becomes: how do you build it, in yourself and your company? It's developable — not a gift you either have or don't — but it's built the slow way, through exposure and friction, not bought.

1. **Consume the best, deliberately and constantly.** Taste is pattern recognition, and you can only recognize patterns you've been exposed to. Read the best writing in and out of your field. Study the work that lands and ask *why.* The people with the sharpest judgment are, without exception, the ones who've paid the most attention to excellent work. There's no shortcut around the input.
2. **Practice the cut, not just the make.** Taste lives in subtraction — in knowing what to remove. Make a habit of taking competent work (yours, AI's, anyone's) and asking "what would make this sharper? what's generic here? what's the one true thing buried in the average?" The muscle is discernment, and discernment is built by judging, repeatedly, with feedback.
3. **Develop a point of view about quality.** You can't judge without a standard, and a standard requires opinions — about what good writing does, what your brand should and shouldn't sound like, what "specific" means versus "average." Vague standards produce vague judgment. The sharper your articulated sense of good, the sharper your ability to recognize it (and reject its absence) in real time.
4. **Make taste the role, not an afterthought.** As production gets cheap, the highest-value person in the room shifts from the one who can *make* the most to the one who can *judge* the best — the editor, the curator, the one with the standard. Organize the work around that. The bottleneck isn't drafting anymore. It's someone with taste deciding what's worth keeping.

The reassuring thing in all of this, if you're a person who has spent years developing judgment, is that your advantage just got more valuable, not less. Everyone fearing that AI commoditizes their skill has it backwards if their skill was ever really *taste* rather than mere production. The production part of your work, yes, that commoditized. But the discernment — the eye, the standard, the knowing-what's-good — that's now the scarce thing in a world drowning in competent average. The flood of generic content doesn't threaten the person with taste. It makes them indispensable, because someone has to be able to tell what's worth reading, and the machines can't.

So build the moat that doesn't erode. Not the ability to make more — that race is over and everyone tied. The ability to know what's worth making. In a world where anyone can produce anything, the person who knows what's good wins, and keeps winning, because that's the one advantage the tools handed *up* the chain instead of away.
