The story you tell about who you are was mostly written by someone who didn't have all the information yet. They wrote it young, or scared, or in the middle of a situation they were just trying to survive. They wrote it to make sense of a particular set of circumstances, and then those circumstances changed, and the story stayed — because nobody told you the story was a draft, and drafts are allowed to be rewritten.

I work with founders and executives on brand language, but the thing underneath that work is almost always personal, because a person's professional story and their sense of self are the same document with different fonts. And the most common thing I see is someone living inside an old draft of themselves. The career-changer still introducing themselves by the job they left. The founder still apologizing for the credential they don't have. The woman in the middle of becoming someone new who keeps narrating from the chapter she's already finished. They're reading from a draft. They've just forgotten they're allowed to hold the pen.

> You are not the first draft you wrote of yourself under pressure. You are the author, and the author gets to revise. The only reason the old version still runs your introductions is that no one ever told you the page was yours to change.

Here's the reframe I want to offer, because it changes everything about how reinvention feels. We treat reinvention as *starting over* — burning it down, becoming someone unrecognizable, a clean slate. That framing makes it terrifying, because starting over means everything before was wasted. But that's not what's actually happening when someone reinvents. They're not starting over. They're *editing.* The material is all still there — the scars, the detours, the failed thing, the years that felt like a waste. Reinvention doesn't delete them. It recontextualizes them. Same facts, new meaning. That's not a blank page. That's a rewrite, and a rewrite keeps the good lines.

## Editing the Draft

If reinvention is editing rather than erasing, then the move is not to throw out your history. It's to read it again with a different question in mind. A few of the edits I've watched change how someone carries themselves:

- **The detour becomes the throughline.** The thing you thought was off-track — the wrong industry, the years that "didn't count," the pivot — is usually the exact thing that makes your current work non-generic. In the first draft it was a digression. In the edit, it's the reason you see what others miss. Same fact. The meaning is what you revised.
- **The wound becomes the credential.** You don't have to perform a tidy, healed version of your story. The hard thing you went through is often the most load-bearing part of why people trust you — *because* you survived it, not in spite of it. The edit isn't hiding the wound. It's understanding what it qualifies you to say.
- **The apology gets cut.** Read your own introduction and find the apology hiding in it — the "just," the "kind of," the credential you preemptively excuse. That's first-draft language, written by the scared version. Cut it. You don't owe the room an explanation for the route you took to get here.
- **The tense changes.** First drafts narrate in the past — who you were, what happened to you. The edit moves you into the present and future tense — who you're becoming, what you're building. That grammatical shift is most of the psychological one. You stop being a thing that happened and start being a thing in motion.

None of this is about lying or spin or "manifesting" your way past reality. The facts of your life are the facts. Editing doesn't change what happened; it changes what the facts are *for.* And that's not dishonest — it's the most honest thing you can do, because the scared first draft was never the truest version. It was just the earliest one, written before you knew how the chapter would turn out.

You've finished chapters the first draft couldn't have predicted. You have information now that the author who wrote your introduction didn't have. That's not a reason to feel behind. It's a reason to pick up the pen. The draft was never meant to be final. It was meant to be revised by the person you became while living it.

So revise it. You were always the author. You just had to believe in the plot you were in.
