Somewhere in your history is a story you used to tell and stopped. The real one — why you actually started this, the moment underneath the moment, the personal reason you'd give a friend at 1 a.m. but would never put on the About page. You stopped telling it because it felt too personal, or not professional enough, or you decided the market wanted the polished version. So you swapped the true origin for a sanitized one — "I saw a gap in the market," "I'd always been passionate about this space" — and the realest, most magnetic part of your story went quiet.

That buried story is almost always your best positioning, and you traded it for something safer and weaker. I see this constantly. A founder's official narrative is a competent, forgettable sequence of professional milestones, and then somewhere in our second conversation they mention — offhand, almost apologizing — the actual reason. The thing that happened to them. The frustration they lived through. The person they were trying to become. And it's electric. It's specific, it's human, it's the thing no competitor could copy because it happened to *them.* And they've kept it off every page they own, because it felt like oversharing.

> The part of your story you're slightly embarrassed to tell is usually the part that does the most work. Polish makes you credible; the unpolished true thing makes you *chosen.* You buried the magnet and kept the business card.

There's a reason this happens, and it's worth naming so you can resist it. As businesses mature, they professionalize, and professionalization has a sanding effect — it smooths the specific into the generic in the name of looking legitimate. The weird, personal, true origin gets replaced by the kind of story that sounds like a press release because press releases feel safe. But safe and forgettable are the same thing here. The sanitized origin story is *credible* and completely inert. It informs and does not move. Nobody ever chose a founder because of a tidy market-gap narrative. They choose because something in the realer story made them feel they were dealing with a particular human who *meant* it.

## Telling It Again

Recovering the story you stopped telling isn't about confession or trauma-dumping on your homepage. It's about finding the true throughline and letting it carry the weight the polished version can't. A few ways back to it:

- **Answer the 1 a.m. version.** If a friend asked, late and honest, "why do you *actually* do this?" — what would you say? That answer, lightly edited for a public room, is usually the origin story you should be telling. The press-release version is what you say when you're protecting yourself. The 1 a.m. version is what connects.
- **Find the moment, not the milestone.** Milestones ("founded the company in 2019") inform. Moments ("I watched a brilliant founder lose a deal because she couldn't explain what she did, and I couldn't stop thinking about it") connect. There's a specific scene underneath your work. Tell the scene.
- **Keep the part you want to cut.** The detail you're tempted to remove for being too personal is frequently the load-bearing one — the thing that makes the story unmistakably yours instead of anyone's. Embarrassment is often just proximity to the truth. Notice what you're reflexively sanding off, and put it back.
- **Let it cost you a little.** A true story that connects with the right people will, by being specific, fail to connect with some others — and might reveal something real about you. That exposure is the price of resonance. The perfectly safe story connects with no one, precisely because it risked nothing.

I'm not arguing for relentless oversharing or for turning every piece of copy into a personal essay. There's judgment in this — what's relevant, what's the right room, what actually serves the reader versus what just serves your need to be seen. But most founders are nowhere near that line. They're miles on the other side of it, telling a story so professionally smoothed that nothing about it could possibly stick. The risk for almost everyone isn't oversharing. It's the slow disappearance of the one part of the story that was ever going to make someone care.

So go find the story you stopped telling. The real one. The one you'd tell a friend but took off the page. It's probably the best thing you ever wrote about yourself — and you've been hiding it because it was true.
