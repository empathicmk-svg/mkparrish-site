The better you understand your own work, the worse you tend to write about it. This sounds backwards, and it is the single most reliable pattern I see across expert founders. The person who has spent ten years inside a problem cannot remember what it was like to not understand it — and so they write for the version of the reader who already knows what they know, which is to say, for no one. Expertise doesn't make your copy clearer. Past a certain point, it actively makes it worse.

Psychologists call this the curse of knowledge: once you know something, you can't un-know it, and you lose the ability to imagine not knowing it. For an expert, this is a constant, invisible tax on every sentence. You skip the step that seems obvious to you — and it's the exact step your reader needed. You use the term of art that took you years to internalize, forgetting it took you years. You compress three logical leaps into one because you can make them instantly, and you leave the reader stranded at the first gap. None of this feels like a mistake while you're doing it. It feels like being efficient.

> The curse of knowledge is why your smartest people write your most confusing copy. They're not bad writers. They've just lost access to the one thing the reader has — not knowing yet — which is the exact vantage point good writing has to be written from.

Here's the cruel part: the reader doesn't experience your expert copy as "too advanced for me." They experience it as *confusing*, and confusion reads as a problem with you, not with them. A prospect who can't follow your homepage doesn't think "I should learn more." They think "I don't get what this does," and they leave. The expertise you were trying to signal becomes the thing that loses the deal. You sounded smart to the three people who already understood you and lost everyone else, which is most of the market.

## Writing From the Other Side of the Gap

The fix is not to dumb things down — that's the wrong frame and it produces condescending copy. The fix is to *rebuild the bridge* you've forgotten you crossed. A few ways to do it:

1. **Find your forgotten beginner.** Remember, specifically, what confused *you* when you started. The question you were too embarrassed to ask. The thing that finally made it click. That memory is your most valuable writing asset, because it's the reader's current location. Write from there, toward where you are now.
2. **Name the step you want to skip.** When you catch yourself thinking "obviously, then X" — stop. The word "obviously" is the curse talking. The step that feels too obvious to state is almost always the one the reader needed stated. Put it back.
3. **Read it to someone outside the field.** Not for praise — for the exact moment their eyes glaze. That glaze is data: it marks the precise sentence where you left them behind. You cannot feel that moment from the inside; you have to watch it happen on someone else's face.
4. **Translate every term of art once.** You don't have to abandon the precise vocabulary of your field — sometimes it's load-bearing. But the first time you use it, translate it in plain language, so the reader who doesn't have it isn't locked out. Precision and accessibility aren't enemies; pairing them is the whole craft.

There's a deeper reason this matters for positioning specifically. The thing that makes you expert — the deep, hard-won understanding — is exactly what your buyer is paying for. But they can only buy it if they can *perceive* it, and they can only perceive it through writing they can follow. Clear copy isn't the opposite of deep expertise. It's the only delivery mechanism expertise has. The expert who can also be understood beats the expert who can't, every single time, because the second one's knowledge never makes it across the gap.

This is, incidentally, a lot of what a good outside writer actually does for an expert business. Not "make it sound better" — *carry the reader across the gap the expert can no longer see.* The writer's slight distance from the subject, the thing that might look like a disadvantage, is the advantage. They still remember not knowing. They can feel where the bridge is missing.

So before you publish the thing you wrote about your own deep work, ask the uncomfortable question: am I writing this for the reader who's just arriving, or for the version of myself who already knows? If it's the second one, you've written something impressive and useless. Go back and build the bridge. The knowledge is worth nothing the reader can't reach.
