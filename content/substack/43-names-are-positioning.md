What you call something determines how it's understood before anyone reads a word of explanation. The name of your product, your offer, your feature, your company — it's not a label you slap on after the real work; it's the first and most repeated piece of positioning you'll ever ship. A name travels everywhere your copy can't follow: into conversations you're not in, into the sentence a customer uses to recommend you, into the split-second a prospect decides whether this is worth understanding. Names are positioning, compressed to a word or two, and most people treat them as an afterthought, which is why most names do nothing.

Consider how much work a name does that you never see. When someone hears the name of your offering, they immediately slot it into a mental category — "oh, it's one of *those*" — and that category determines their expectations, their comparisons, and their willingness to pay. A name that lands you in the wrong category fights an uphill battle every time; a name that claims the right category does free work on every impression. "The Reinvention Workbook" tells you what it is, who it's for, and what it'll do, instantly. "Catalyst Pro" tells you nothing and makes you work. The first name positions; the second just sits there being clever.

> A name is the only piece of positioning that shows up in conversations you'll never be part of. It's what a customer says when they recommend you, and what a prospect categorizes you by before reading a word. Get it wrong and every other word of copy is correcting it.

This is where the most common naming mistake comes from: optimizing for *clever* instead of *clear*. Founders fall in love with names that are evocative, abstract, or wordplay-driven — names that feel creative and ownable. But clever names usually require explanation, and a name that needs explaining has failed at the one job a name has, which is to communicate without you there. The market doesn't reward the cleverest name. It rewards the clearest one — the name that makes the right person instantly understand what this is and feel it's for them. Clarity isn't the boring choice; it's the strategic one.

## Naming as Positioning Work

Treating naming as positioning rather than decoration changes how you do it. A few principles:

1. **Name for the category you want to be in.** A name implies a category, and the category implies the comparison set and the price. Decide what you want to be compared to, then name toward it. If you want premium positioning, don't name like a commodity; if you're creating a new category, the name may need to *teach* it (see: offers that name the problem they solve).
2. **Prefer clear over clever — clarity is ownable too.** "Write Yourself Into the Room" is clear *and* distinctive. You don't have to choose between understood and memorable; the best names are both, with clarity load-bearing and personality on top. If you must trade, trade away clever. A clear name compounds; a clever-but-opaque one needs a lifetime of explanation.
3. **Test it in the wild sentence.** Say the name inside the sentence a customer would actually use: "Oh, you should check out ___, it's the thing that ___." If the name makes that sentence work, it's doing its job. If you have to add three clauses to make it make sense, the name is offloading its work onto your copy.
4. **Name the offer to name the value.** For services and products, naming the *outcome or transformation* often beats naming the mechanism. "The Rewrite," "The Brand Voice Playbook" — these names carry the value proposition in the title. The name becomes a tiny, repeatable pitch that travels on its own.

There's a reason naming is some of the highest-leverage work you can do, and it's about repetition. You write a homepage once; the name gets repeated thousands of times — by you, your team, your customers, your critics. A small improvement in a name compounds across every one of those repetitions, while a small improvement in a paragraph most people never reach does not. Fixing a name is often the single cheapest, highest-return positioning move available, precisely because the name is the most-used word in your entire business.

And yet it's treated as a last-minute decision, made under deadline, optimized for "available domain" and "sounds cool in the meeting." That's backwards. The name deserves the same rigor as the positioning — because it *is* the positioning, in its most distilled and most-traveled form. The name is the argument you make when you're not in the room, and you're not in the room far more often than you are.

So look at what you call things — the company, the product, the offer, the feature people actually care about. Does each name do positioning work, or does it just sit there needing explanation? The name is the cheapest leverage you have and the most repeated word you own. Make it carry the meaning, so your copy doesn't have to spend its life correcting it.
