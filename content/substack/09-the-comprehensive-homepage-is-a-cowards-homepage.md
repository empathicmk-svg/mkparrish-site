The homepage that tries to say everything is not thorough. It is afraid. Behind every wall of feature bullets, every "we also do," every section added because someone in a meeting worried we weren't *covering* it, is a founder who could not bring themselves to bet on one thing. Comprehensiveness looks like generosity — *look how much we offer* — but it is usually the opposite. It is the refusal to choose, dressed up as completeness, and the reader feels the cowardice even when they can't name it.

I'll say the uncomfortable part plainly, because it's the whole point: every additional thing you put on the page makes the most important thing harder to see. Attention is not additive. A reader does not absorb your homepage like a database, accumulating every claim into a complete picture. They skim, they grab one or two impressions, and they leave. If you gave them fourteen things, you didn't give them fourteen impressions. You gave them a blur, and a blur converts no one. The page that says six things says nothing, because the reader can't tell which of the six you actually meant.

> Adding to a homepage feels safe and subtracting feels risky, so founders add. But the page is not graded on what it contains. It's graded on what a distracted human remembers ten seconds after they leave it — and you can't remember a list.

The reason this happens is structural, and worth naming so you can catch yourself doing it. Internal pressure always pushes toward addition. Sales wants the enterprise logos *and* the SMB pricing. Product wants the new feature above the fold. The founder wants the origin story, the values, the mission, and the three differentiators. Every one of those requests is individually reasonable. Collectively they produce a page optimized for the people who built it and useless to the person it's for. No one in the building is assigned to defend the reader's attention, so the reader's attention is the thing that gets spent.

## The Discipline of One Strong Thing

Editing a homepage is not about making it shorter for its own sake. It's about deciding what the page is *for* and then having the nerve to let the rest go quiet. A few rules I hold clients to:

1. **One job above the fold.** The top of the page does exactly one thing: make the right person think *this is for me, and I want to know more.* Not buy. Not understand the full product. One thought, one job. Everything competing for that space is demoted or cut.
2. **If everything is bold, nothing is.** Emphasis is a budget, not a setting. You get to make one thing the loudest. The moment the page has three "most important" sections, it has zero, and the reader's eye gives up trying to find the point.
3. **The second-best idea is the enemy of the best one.** Cutting a weak section is easy. The hard cuts — the ones that actually sharpen a page — are the *good* ideas you remove so the *best* idea can stand alone and be seen. Killing your darlings isn't about killing the bad ones. It's about the good ones you sacrifice for focus.
4. **What you leave out is a message too.** A page that confidently covers one thing and ignores ten others reads as a company that knows exactly what it's for. A page that covers everything reads as a company hedging. Restraint is legible. Buyers can feel the difference between "we chose this" and "we couldn't decide."

The test I use is brutal and clarifying: if you could keep only one section of your homepage and delete the rest, which would you keep? Whatever you just answered — that's the page. Everything else is supporting it or burying it, and most of it is burying it. The exercise feels like loss. It is actually the first time the page has had a chance to land.

None of this means your company only does one thing, or that the other offerings don't matter. It means the homepage is not a catalog. It's an argument, and an argument has a thesis, and a thesis you've surrounded with thirteen other theses is no longer a thesis — it's a table of contents nobody asked for. Save the completeness for the pages a committed reader goes looking for. The front door gets one sentence's worth of courage.

Pick the one thing. Let it stand alone. The empty space around it isn't wasted — it's the spotlight.
