Founders treat pricing as a math problem. Cost-plus-margin, or competitor-minus-ten-percent, or whatever-the-spreadsheet-says — a number arrived at through calculation and then dropped onto the page, naked, to fend for itself. And then they're surprised when prospects flinch, negotiate, or ghost. But the prospect isn't reacting to the number. They're reacting to the number *plus everything around it* — which is almost nothing, because the founder spent all their energy choosing the figure and none framing it. Price is not a number. Price is a sentence, and most founders write the number and forget the sentence.

Here's what I mean concretely. "$10,000" means nothing on its own. It is not expensive or cheap; it is a quantity with no reference frame. "$10,000 — about what you'd pay one mid-level hire for three weeks, except this doesn't leave in eighteen months" is a different price entirely, even though the number is identical. The figure didn't change. The sentence around it did, and the sentence is what the buyer actually evaluates. A price with no frame defaults to the most expensive frame the buyer can imagine, because you left the framing to them, and they're not on your side.

> A number is data. A price is a story about value, and you are either telling that story or letting the buyer tell it to themselves. They will almost always tell themselves the version where you cost too much, because that's the version that protects their wallet.

This is why the same service sells for wildly different amounts depending entirely on the language around it — a fact that offends people's sense of fairness but is simply how valuation works in the human mind. We do not assess worth in a vacuum. We assess it relative to anchors, alternatives, and the frame we're handed. Hand the buyer no frame and they'll reach for the cheapest comparison available ("I could get a freelancer for a fraction of that"). Hand them the right frame and the same price reads as obvious, even generous. The framing is not manipulation. It's just refusing to let the worst-case comparison win by default.

## Writing the Sentence Around the Number

Pricing copy is some of the highest-leverage writing you'll ever do, and almost no one drafts it deliberately. Here's how to actually write the sentence the price lives in.

1. **Anchor before you reveal.** The number should never be the first quantity the reader encounters. Before the price, establish the stakes — the cost of the problem, the value of the outcome, the price of the obvious alternative. By the time they reach your figure, it should feel small relative to what you've already put in their head. (See post 02: most copy problems are worth more than the rewrite costs. That sentence is anchoring.)
2. **Compare to the right thing.** Buyers will compare your price to *something* — the only question is what. If you don't choose the comparison, they'll pick the cheapest one. So pick it for them: not "versus a freelancer" but "versus the cost of the leak you currently have," or "versus one bad hire." Choose the anchor that makes your price the rational choice.
3. **Name what the price protects against.** Often the strongest frame isn't the upside — it's the downside avoided. "This is what it costs to stop guessing." "This is cheaper than being wrong for another year." Loss framing lands harder than gain framing, because people work harder to avoid a loss than to secure an equivalent gain.
4. **Let the price signal the position.** A premium number, stated plainly and unapologetically, is itself a claim: *this is not the commodity option, and if you're shopping on price you're in the wrong place.* Discounting and over-justifying both leak the opposite signal. Sometimes the most persuasive thing you can do with a price is state it and stop talking.

The mistake underneath all of this is thinking the number does the work. The number is just the punchline. The setup — the frame, the anchor, the comparison, the stakes — is what determines whether the punchline lands as "reasonable" or "expensive." And the setup is *writing*. It's the most commercially important paragraph on your sales page, and it's usually the one nobody drafted, because everyone was arguing about the figure.

Go look at how your price currently appears. Is it a naked number, dropped on the page to defend itself? Or is it a sentence — framed, anchored, sitting next to the right comparison? If it's naked, you're not too expensive. You're just unframed, and you're letting the buyer write the story you should have written for them.
