There are two versions of your bio. There's the one you have — careful, hedged, slightly shrunk, written to make sure no one could accuse you of thinking too highly of yourself. And there's the one you're afraid of — the accurate one, the one that says the true thing about how good you actually are, the one you've never let yourself write because it feels like bragging. This is about the second one. You should write it. The fear standing between you and it is not protecting you from arrogance. It's protecting you from being chosen.

Most capable people systematically under-claim. They round their own value down. They lead with the modest framing, bury the strongest fact, add the deflecting "just" — "I just help people with their copy" — and call it humility. But it isn't humility, not really. Humility is an accurate assessment of yourself that includes your limitations. What I'm describing is something else: a *distortion downward,* a habit of stating less than what's true because the truth feels uncomfortable to say out loud. And the reader can't tell the difference between someone who isn't very good and someone who's good but won't say so. Both sound small. The under-claim costs you the exact thing the bio was for.

> Stating clearly that you're excellent at the specific thing you're excellent at is not arrogance. It's accuracy. Arrogance is claiming what isn't true. Refusing to claim what *is* true isn't modesty — it's just a quieter way of misrepresenting yourself.

The fear has a logic, and it's worth dismantling. We're taught — especially women, especially anyone who's been on the receiving end of "who does she think she is" — that confident self-description invites punishment. Safer to undersell and let people discover you're good. But "let them discover it" only works if they stick around long enough to discover it, and they won't, because the underselling bio gave them no reason to. The market doesn't reward the modest and patient with eventual recognition. It rewards the clear. The person who accurately states their strength gets the meeting; the person hiding an identical strength behind "just" does not.

## Writing the Braver Version

Writing the bio you're afraid of is not about inflating yourself into someone you're not. It's about removing the downward distortion so the page finally matches the reality. Here's the work:

1. **Write down the truest strong sentence about your work — the one you'd never publish.** The one that feels like too much. That sentence, if it's actually true, is probably the one your bio needs. Start there and edit *toward* it, not away from it.
2. **Delete every "just," "kind of," "sort of," and "I try to."** These are the verbal tics of the downward distortion — tiny apologies inserted to soften a claim. Cut them and read it back. Notice the sentence didn't become arrogant. It became accurate. The hedges were never adding truth; they were subtracting it.
3. **Replace modesty with specificity.** The way to claim excellence without sounding like a windbag isn't to hedge — it's to be concrete. "I'm a great writer" is a vague boast. "I've rewritten homepages that doubled demo bookings" is a specific claim that's stronger *and* more credible. Specificity lets you be bold without being vague, which is the combination that reads as earned rather than inflated.
4. **Run the accuracy test, not the comfort test.** The question for every line isn't "does this feel comfortable to say?" (the strong lines never will) — it's "is this *true*?" If it's true, your discomfort is not a reason to cut it. Your discomfort is just the feeling of telling the truth about yourself, which you're out of practice at.

There's a real boundary here, and I want to honor it: the goal is accuracy, not inflation. Claiming things that aren't true is its own failure mode, and a worse one — it reads as desperation and collapses the moment it's tested. The brave bio isn't the *biggest* possible bio. It's the *truest* one, written without the reflexive shrinking. For a small number of people, the work really is to claim less. For almost everyone I meet, it's the opposite. The honest version of their value would feel, to them, like bragging — which tells you exactly how far down they've been rounding.

So write the one you're afraid of. Not the inflated one — the accurate one, the one with the strong true sentence you've never let yourself say. Read it back and feel the discomfort, and then recognize the discomfort for what it is: not a warning that you've gone too far, but the unfamiliar sensation of finally telling the truth about how good you are.
