Teardowns are the most useful thing I publish, because positioning advice in the abstract is easy to nod at and impossible to apply. "Be specific." "Find your differentiator." Sure. The work is in the sentences — in seeing a real before-and-after and understanding *why* the second version converts and the first one doesn't. So once a month, paid subscribers get one: a real homepage, anonymized, rewritten line by line, with the reasoning exposed at every step.

This month's patient is a B2B workflow-software company. Mid-stage, real traction, a homepage written across three years and four hands. The kind of page that isn't *bad* — it's competent, it's clean, it would pass a marketing review — which is exactly why it's worth tearing down. Bad pages are easy. Competent-but-generic pages are the ones quietly leaking conversion while looking fine. They're the common case, and they're the expensive one.

Here's the hero section as it stands today (details changed, structure intact):

> **The modern platform for high-performing teams.**
> Streamline your workflows, eliminate busywork, and unlock your team's full potential — all in one place. Trusted by industry leaders.
> *[Get Started] [Book a Demo]*

Read it and notice the feeling: nothing. It's frictionless and forgettable. Every phrase is true, professional, and completely transferable to any of a hundred competitors. There is not one word in that hero that only this company could have written, which means the hero is doing zero positioning work. It's paying the category cover charge and calling it a homepage.

> The goal of a teardown is not to make the copy "better" in some abstract, stylistic sense. It's to make the copy *yours* — to replace every transferable sentence with one a competitor couldn't say without lying. Better writing is a side effect. Defensible positioning is the point.

## The Teardown

Let's go line by line. For each one: what it says, what's actually wrong, and the rewrite — with the reasoning, so you can run the same passes on your own page.

**Line 1 — "The modern platform for high-performing teams."**

The single most common B2B headline shape there is, and it's three category words stacked: *modern, platform, high-performing teams.* "Modern" is a claim every competitor makes and none can own. "Platform" tells the reader nothing about what it does. "High-performing teams" is the audience flattered into meaninglessness — no one self-identifies as a low-performing team, so it excludes no one and therefore targets no one.

The diagnostic question: *what does this company actually do, for whom, that's different?* In the brief, the answer was sharp — it kills the status-update meeting by turning project chatter into an automatic written digest, aimed at remote teams drowning in syncs. None of that specificity survived into the headline. The headline retreated to safety the moment it hit the page.

> **Rewrite:** *Kill the status meeting. Your team's updates, written for you.*

That headline excludes people (it's for teams who hate syncs), makes a claim a generic competitor can't comfortably copy (most don't lead with "kill the meeting"), and is concrete enough to picture. It winces a little. That's the signal it's doing work.

**Line 2 — "Streamline your workflows, eliminate busywork, and unlock your team's full potential — all in one place."**

Three verbs from the category dictionary (*streamline, eliminate, unlock*) and the tell of an unfocused product: *all in one place.* "All in one place" is what you say when you can't decide which one thing matters most, so you gesture at breadth instead. Breadth doesn't sell on a hero; it dilutes. And "unlock your team's full potential" is the kind of phrase that evaporates on contact — the reader's eye slides off it because it has no edge to catch on.

The fix is to replace abstraction with the specific mechanism. Not "streamline workflows" — say the actual thing the product does that produces the feeling of streamlining.

> **Rewrite:** *It reads your team's messages, pulls out what changed, and sends everyone a clean written update every morning. No one has to ask "what's the latest?" again.*

Notice this subhead does the opposite of the original: it gets *more* specific, not less, and the specificity is what makes it believable. Vague benefit ("unlock potential") asks for trust the page hasn't earned. Concrete mechanism ("reads messages, pulls out what changed") earns the trust by showing the work.

**Line 3 — "Trusted by industry leaders."**

The emptiest sentence on the internet. It's social proof with the proof removed. "Industry leaders" names no one, so it proves nothing — the reader can't verify it and so doesn't credit it. If you have real logos, show them. If you have a real number, use it. If you have neither yet, this line is worse than nothing, because it advertises that you reached for credibility and came back empty-handed.

> **Rewrite (if you have the proof):** *Used by 1,200 remote teams, including [3 real, recognizable logos].*
> **Rewrite (if you don't, yet):** Cut it. Replace with one specific customer sentence: *"We deleted three standing meetings in the first month." — [Name, Title, Company].* One real voice beats a hundred anonymous "industry leaders."

**The CTAs — "[Get Started] [Book a Demo]"**

Two co-equal buttons is a decision the page is refusing to make. Which one do you actually want them to click? Co-equal CTAs split attention and lower the click rate on both. Pick the primary action, make it loud, and demote the secondary one to a quiet text link.

> **Rewrite:** Primary button: *See a sample digest →* (lower-commitment than "Get Started," more concrete than "Book a Demo," and it shows the product instead of asking for a calendar slot). Secondary, as a small link beneath: *or book a walkthrough.*

### What the whole hero becomes

> **Kill the status meeting. Your team's updates, written for you.**
> It reads your team's messages, pulls out what changed, and sends everyone a clean written update every morning. No one has to ask "what's the latest?" again.
> *See a sample digest →* · or book a walkthrough
> *Used by 1,200 remote teams, including [logos].*

Same company. Same product. Same length, roughly. The difference is that every line in the second version is *theirs* — specific, defensible, impossible to paste onto a competitor's site without it becoming a lie. The first version was a competent assembly of category language. The second is a position.

### The five passes, so you can run this yourself

1. **The transferable-line pass.** Highlight every sentence a competitor could use unchanged. Rewrite each toward something only you could say.
2. **The abstraction-to-mechanism pass.** Every vague benefit ("streamline," "unlock," "optimize") gets replaced with the concrete thing the product actually does to produce it.
3. **The proof pass.** Every credibility claim either gets real evidence attached or gets cut. No anonymous "industry leaders."
4. **The one-job pass.** The hero does exactly one thing. Anything competing with that job is demoted down the page or removed.
5. **The single-CTA pass.** One primary action, loud. Everything else is a quiet secondary link.

Run those five passes on your own hero this week. Most homepages gain more from this single hour than from a month of new traffic — because traffic amplifies whatever the page already does, and right now, for most pages, that's "say something forgettable, confidently."
