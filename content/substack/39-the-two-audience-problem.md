Most B2B copy fails because it's trying to talk to two different people at once and ends up reaching neither. There's the person who *signs* — the executive, the economic buyer, the one who cares about outcomes, risk, and what this does for the business. And there's the person who *uses* — the practitioner, the team member, the one who cares whether it'll actually work, whether it'll make their day better or worse. These two read your homepage with completely different questions, and copy written for one tends to actively repel the other. Solve this badly and your message is mush. Solve it well and you've unlocked the room.

The classic failure runs in both directions. Write for the executive — "drive transformational ROI across your organization" — and the practitioner rolls their eyes, because it sounds like a vendor who's never done the actual work and will be painful to use. Write for the practitioner — deep in features and technical specifics — and the executive can't tell what business problem you solve or why they should care, so they never engage. The buyer and the user have different fears, different vocabularies, and different definitions of "good." A single undifferentiated message splits the difference and lands with neither.

> The person who signs the contract and the person who lives with the product are not the same person, and they are not persuaded by the same things. Copy that forgets this is always talking past at least one of them — usually the one you needed most.

What makes this genuinely hard is that you often need *both* to say yes. The executive won't buy something their team will revolt against; the team can't buy without the executive's budget. So you can't just pick one audience and ignore the other — you have to move both, often within the same page. The mistake is thinking that means saying everything to everyone. It doesn't. It means *structuring* your message so each audience finds what they need, fast, without the other's content getting in their way.

## Writing for Both Without Mush

The goal is not to blend the two messages into one gray average. It's to give each audience a clear path. A few ways to do it:

1. **Lead with the outcome, support with the mechanism.** The headline and top of the page should speak the *buyer's* language — the business outcome, the risk removed, the result. Then, as the page goes deeper, shift toward the *user's* language — how it works, why it won't be painful, the specifics that earn a practitioner's trust. Outcome up top earns the click; mechanism below earns the confidence. Both, in order.
2. **Let the practitioner overhear the buyer's pitch, and vice versa.** Well-structured copy lets each audience benefit from seeing the other addressed. The user, reading the outcome-level pitch, thinks "good, leadership will get why this matters." The executive, skimming the practitioner-level proof, thinks "good, the team will actually adopt this." You're not hiding either message — you're sequencing them so each reassures both.
3. **Name the user's pain in the buyer's pitch.** The most persuasive thing to an executive is often evidence that you understand their *team's* real experience — because adoption is their hidden fear. "Your team has rejected three tools that looked good in the demo" speaks to the buyer *through* empathy for the user. One sentence, both audiences.
4. **Use proof that covers both.** A testimonial that has the executive's outcome ("cut our sales cycle") and the practitioner's relief ("and the team actually wanted to use it") does double duty. Seek out proof that speaks to both fears at once; it's worth more than two separate quotes.

The deeper strategic move is to be clear-eyed about *who decides.* In some sales, the executive drives and the user has veto power; in others, the user champions and the executive approves budget. The weighting changes which audience leads your message — but it's rarely "ignore one entirely." Map the actual decision: who has to be excited, who has to be unafraid, who signs, who can kill it. Then sequence your copy to move each of them in the order they encounter the page.

This is, fundamentally, a positioning question disguised as a copy question. Before you can write for two audiences well, you have to *decide* what you are to each of them — the business outcome you represent to the buyer, and the daily-life improvement you represent to the user. Those are two different value propositions for the same product, and a strong page makes both, clearly, without contradiction. Most weak B2B pages have made neither, which is why they sound like they're talking to a committee that doesn't exist.

So read your homepage twice — once as the executive who'll sign, once as the practitioner who'll live with it. Does each one find what they need? Or does each one find the other's content and bounce? If it's mush, you don't have a writing problem. You have two audiences and one undifferentiated message, and the fix is structure, not more words.
