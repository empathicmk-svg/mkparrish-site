The instinct, when you write for your business, is to hold the door open as wide as it will go. You don't know exactly who's going to walk through, so you write something roomy enough for all of them. You soften the specifics. You hedge the bold claim so it won't alienate the segment you haven't met yet. You write "for teams of all sizes" because what if a big one shows up. The logic feels like prudence: don't narrow, don't exclude, don't shrink the addressable market with my own words.

It is the single most expensive instinct in marketing, and it is backwards.

Copy written for everyone reaches no one, because no one has ever felt *seen* by a sentence designed not to leave anyone out. Recognition — the jolt of "this is about me, specifically" — is the thing that makes a reader stop scrolling and start trusting. And recognition is impossible to manufacture at the level of everyone. You can only be recognized by a person if you were willing to describe a person. The wide door is so wide that no one notices it's a door at all. They walk past it the way they walk past every other wide, soft, inoffensive thing on the internet.

> Specificity is not the thing that shrinks your market. Specificity is the thing that makes your market notice you exist. You cannot be chosen by people who never felt addressed — and no one feels addressed by copy written to avoid addressing anyone.

Here is the math the fear gets wrong. You imagine that narrowing from "everyone" to "second-time founders who got burned by their first agency" cuts your market by ninety percent. In raw headcount, sure. But you were never converting "everyone" anyway — you were converting a tiny, diffuse fraction of everyone at a miserable rate, because nothing you said landed with enough force to move anyone. Narrow the message and your conversion rate among the people who *do* fit climbs steeply, because now the copy hits them where they live. A high conversion rate on a defined audience beats a near-zero rate on an undefined one, every time, and it isn't close. You're not shrinking the market. You're trading a wide puddle for a deep well.

## Write for One, Sell to Many

The technique that fixes this is almost embarrassingly simple, and nearly everyone resists it for the same emotional reason: it feels like leaving money on the table. It is not. It is how you pick the money up.

- **Name one reader and write the whole piece to them.** Not a persona doc with demographics. A specific human, ideally a real one you've worked with — their situation, their skepticism, the exact thing keeping them up at 2 a.m. Write to that person as if they're the only one who will ever read it.
- **Let the specificity do the qualifying.** When you describe one person's situation precisely, two things happen at once: the people who match feel seen and lean in, and the people who don't self-select out before they waste your sales team's time. That second effect is a feature. Sharp copy is a filter, not just a magnet.
- **Trust the eavesdropper.** Here's the part that dissolves the fear: people who aren't your exact "one" still convert when they overhear copy written for someone specific. They think, *that's not quite me, but these people clearly get it* — and specificity reads as competence. Vague copy written at everyone reads as the opposite. You reach the many by writing for the one, not by writing at the all.
- **Cut every "and/or" hedge.** "For founders and marketers and operators" is three audiences in a trench coat. Pick the one who feels the pain most acutely and aim there. The others will still hear you. They just won't be the ones you're writing the sentence to please.

The founders who can't do this usually aren't confused about the technique. They're afraid. Narrowing feels like a bet, and it is one — you are wagering that going deep with a defined audience beats going shallow with an undefined one. I have watched that bet pay out across enough engagements to tell you it is the safest wager in the building. The risk was never in narrowing. The risk was in the wide soft door no one ever noticed.

Pick your one reader. Write to them like the others aren't watching. The others are watching, and that's exactly why it works.
