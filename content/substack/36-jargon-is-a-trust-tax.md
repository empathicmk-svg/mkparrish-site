Every piece of jargon on your website is a small toll you charge the reader, and most readers refuse to pay it. "Synergistic, end-to-end, AI-powered orchestration layer." "Holistic, scalable, best-in-class solutions." Each of these phrases asks the reader to do work — to decode, to translate, to guess what you actually mean — in exchange for nothing. And the reader, who has options, simply declines. They don't write you an email asking for clarification. They close the tab. Jargon isn't a credibility signal. It's a trust tax, and you're charging it to the exact people you're trying to win.

The reason people reach for jargon is almost always insecurity dressed as professionalism. The specialized vocabulary feels safe — it signals you belong in the industry, that you know the words, that you're a serious player. But here's what it actually signals to a reader: that you're either hiding behind the language because you can't say the thing plainly, or that you care more about sounding impressive to insiders than being understood by buyers. Both readings cost you. Confidence sounds like clarity. Insecurity sounds like jargon. Readers can tell the difference instantly, even when they can't articulate why.

> Plain language is a power move. It takes more confidence to say "we help you write emails people actually open" than "we provide AI-driven engagement optimization solutions" — because the plain version can be judged, and the jargon version can hide. Hiding reads as weakness.

There's a tell that separates real expertise from performed expertise. Real experts can explain complex things simply, because they understand them deeply enough to find the plain words. People performing expertise reach for jargon precisely because they're not sure they could survive saying it simply — the complexity is a smokescreen. So when a reader hits a wall of jargon, their instinct (often unconscious) is suspicion: *what is this obscuring?* The vocabulary you chose to sound smart makes you sound like you have something to hide.

## Paying Down the Tax

Stripping jargon isn't about sounding casual or dumbing down. It's about removing the toll so the reader can actually get to your value. Here's the work:

- **Run the "say it to a friend" test.** Read a jargon-heavy sentence, then ask: how would I explain this to a smart friend who isn't in my industry, over coffee? That version — the one you'd actually speak — is almost always clearer, warmer, and more persuasive than the one you wrote. Write the coffee version.
- **Replace the category word with the concrete thing.** "Solutions," "platform," "ecosystem," "leverage," "enablement" — these are placeholders for specifics you haven't committed to. Each time you catch one, force yourself to name the actual thing it's standing in for. "Solutions" → *the three problems you actually fix.*
- **Keep the term of art only when it earns its place.** Some specialized words are load-bearing and your audience genuinely uses them — keep those; using them correctly signals real fluency. The test is audience, not abstraction: would your *actual buyer* use this word naturally, or only your competitors? If only competitors, cut it.
- **Watch for jargon that's hiding a weak claim.** Often the most jargon-dense sentence is the one with the least actual content — the vagueness is doing the same hiding the vocabulary is. When you translate it to plain language and it suddenly says nothing, you've found a claim you need to make real, not just reword.

The deeper point is about who you're writing for. Jargon optimizes for the approval of insiders — peers, competitors, the people who'd nod at the conference. Plain language optimizes for the comprehension of buyers — the people who actually pay you. Those are different audiences with opposite needs, and most expert businesses, without realizing it, write for the first group while needing the second. You're impressing the people who will never buy from you and losing the people who would.

So go read your homepage as a tax bill. Every term of art, every "leverage," every "holistic end-to-end" — that's a toll you're charging a reader who has somewhere else to be. Most of them aren't paying it. Drop the tax, say the thing plainly, and watch how much more it sounds like you actually know what you're talking about. Because you do. You were just hiding it.
