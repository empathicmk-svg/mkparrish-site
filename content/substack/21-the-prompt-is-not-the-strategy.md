There's a particular hope I keep running into, and it goes like this: *if I could just find the right prompt, the AI would write the thing I actually need.* The founder has tried generic prompts and gotten generic output, so they've concluded the fix is a better prompt — more context, the right persona, a clever framework someone sold them in a thread. More prompt engineering. And so they pour effort into the prompt, and the output gets marginally less generic and remains fundamentally hollow, because they've misunderstood where the hard part is. The prompt is not the strategy. The prompt is the *typing.* The strategy happens before you open the tool, and no prompt can contain a decision you haven't made.

Here's the thing the prompt-engineering hope gets wrong. A prompt can only transmit thinking that already exists. If you have a sharp, specific, decided point of view, a good prompt will help the model express it faster. But if you *don't* — if the underlying positioning is vague, the audience undefined, the argument unmade — then the prompt has nothing real to transmit, and the model does what it always does with a vacuum: it fills it with the confident average of everything it's read. You can engineer that prompt for a week. You'll get a more elaborate average. The hollowness isn't in the prompt. It's in the absence of a decision the prompt was never capable of making for you.

> AI can execute a point of view. It cannot supply one. When the output feels generic, the problem is almost never the prompt — it's that there was no specific thinking upstream for the prompt to carry, and the model averaged the gap.

I watch founders try to prompt their way to positioning, and it never works, because positioning is a series of *choices* — who you're for, what you're against, the one claim you'll defend — and choices are exactly the thing a model won't make for you. Ask it to choose and it will offer you options, hedge between them, or pick the most statistically common one, which is the most generic one. The model is a brilliant executor of decisions and a terrible maker of them. The moment your prompt requires it to have a stake, a side, a refusal — it has none, so it reaches for the average, and the average is the thing you were trying to escape.

## Doing the Upstream Work First

The reframe that fixes this is simple: do the strategic work *before* you touch the tool, and let the tool execute it *after.* That sequence is the whole game. Here's what "upstream" actually means in practice:

1. **Decide the point of view by hand.** Before any prompt, answer the questions a model can't: Who specifically is this for? What do they currently believe that I'm correcting? What's the one claim only I can make? These are decisions, made by a human with a stake. Write them down. *Now* you have something to prompt with.
2. **Bring the specifics the model can't invent.** The real examples, the actual client story, the number, the contrarian take, the metaphor only you would reach for — these don't live in the training data, because they're *yours.* If your prompt doesn't carry them, the output can't contain them. The specificity has to come from you; the model can only arrange what you feed it.
3. **Use AI for the part it's good at — after the thinking.** Once the point of view exists, the model earns its keep: scaffolding a first draft, restructuring, catching repetition, converting an essay into five formats. That's real leverage, and it's downstream of the decision. (More on exactly where AI helps in a separate piece — but the rule holds: execution, not invention.)
4. **Judge the output against your point of view, not against "is it good writing."** The failure mode is accepting fluent output that's drifted off your actual position because it reads smoothly. Fluent and on-point are different axes. The model optimizes for fluent. You have to hold the line on on-point, because that's the part it can't see.

The uncomfortable implication, for anyone hoping AI will let them skip the hard part: it won't, and the people selling you the prompt that supposedly does are selling you a faster way to produce generic content, which is the one kind of content with no value. The bottleneck in great content was never production speed. It was *having something specific to say,* and that bottleneck is exactly as human and exactly as unautomatable as it was three years ago. The tool got faster. The thinking didn't get optional.

So stop looking for the prompt that will save you. There isn't one — not because the right words don't exist, but because the thing you're missing isn't words. It's a decision. Make the decision first, by hand, the old slow way. Then, and only then, let the machine type.
