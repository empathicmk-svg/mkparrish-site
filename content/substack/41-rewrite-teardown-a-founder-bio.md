The founder bio is the highest-leverage paragraph most people own and the one they hand off to the worst writer they know: themselves, at 11pm, the night before a pitch. It goes on the deck, the speaker page, the LinkedIn, the press kit. Investors read it, prospects read it, partners read it — all of them deciding, in about fifteen seconds, whether to take you seriously. And it almost always opens with "a seasoned entrepreneur with a passion for." So this month's paid teardown is a founder bio: a real one, anonymized, rewritten line by line, with the reasoning exposed and a reusable skeleton at the end.

Here's the patient (details changed, structure and clichés intact):

> *[Name] is a seasoned entrepreneur and visionary leader with over 15 years of experience building innovative companies. Passionate about leveraging technology to drive meaningful change, [Name] has a proven track record of scaling businesses and building high-performing teams. A graduate of [University], [Name] is dedicated to creating products that make a difference. In their spare time, they enjoy hiking, reading, and spending time with family.*

Read it and notice the feeling: you've learned the person exists and nothing else. Every sentence is true of ten thousand founders. There's no specific claim, no point of view, no reason to remember this human over any other. It is a bio-shaped fog. And it's *typical*, which is the opportunity — a specific founder bio is almost unfairly conspicuous in a sea of "visionary leaders."

> A founder bio's job is not to summarize a career. It's to make a stranger think "this is the person to build/back/buy this" in fifteen seconds — and you cannot do that with adjectives anyone could claim. Specificity is the entire game.

## The Teardown

**"[Name] is a seasoned entrepreneur and visionary leader..."**

The opening commits the cardinal sin: it leads with self-applied labels. "Seasoned," "visionary," "leader" are judgments the *reader* is supposed to reach — claiming them yourself does the opposite of earning them. And they're unfalsifiable, so they carry no information. Lead with what you've *done* or *believe*, and let the reader conclude you're visionary.

> **Rewrite:** *[Name] has spent 15 years on one problem: why technically great products lose to worse-marketed competitors.* — Now the first line has a *focus* and an implied point of view. It's specific, and it makes the reader curious instead of skeptical.

**"...with over 15 years of experience building innovative companies."**

"Experience building innovative companies" is filler — every founder claims it. The 15 years is the only fact, and it's buried as a modifier. Make the experience *concrete*: what kind of companies, what was the throughline, what did you actually learn or do?

> **Rewrite:** *Three companies, two exits, and one expensive lesson — the best product rarely wins; the best-positioned one does.* Specifics (three, two) are believable; the "expensive lesson" gives the bio a spine and a worldview.

**"Passionate about leveraging technology to drive meaningful change..."**

The densest cluster of clichés in the bio: "passionate," "leveraging," "meaningful change." It says nothing and signals you've read other bios. "Passionate about" is the empty calories of self-description — show the conviction through a specific stance, don't announce the feeling.

> **Rewrite:** Cut entirely, or replace with the actual belief: *[Name] thinks most startups don't have a product problem — they have a "nobody understands what this does" problem.* A real, slightly contrarian claim does what "passionate about change" pretends to.

**"...has a proven track record of scaling businesses and building high-performing teams."**

"Proven track record" is the tell of an unproven claim — if it were proven, you'd show the proof. Replace the abstraction with one concrete, checkable result.

> **Rewrite:** *[Name]'s last company went from $0 to $8M ARR in three years on a category they had to name themselves.* One real number with a story beats "proven track record" every time.

**"A graduate of [University], dedicated to creating products that make a difference."**

The credential can stay if it's genuinely relevant or impressive to *this* audience — but "dedicated to creating products that make a difference" is pure boilerplate. Cut it. It's the participation trophy of bios.

**"In their spare time, they enjoy hiking, reading, and spending time with family."**

The universal bio closer, and it's not wrong to humanize — but "hiking, reading, family" is so generic it humanizes no one. If you include a personal detail, make it *specific* enough to be memorable: a real, slightly particular thing. Otherwise cut it; a generic personal line adds nothing.

> **Rewrite (if keeping):** *Off the clock, [Name] is rewriting the same 600-word short story they've been failing to finish since 2014.* Specific, human, faintly self-aware — memorable in a way "enjoys reading" never is.

### The rebuilt bio

> *[Name] has spent 15 years on one problem: why technically great products lose to worse-marketed competitors. Three companies, two exits, and one expensive lesson — the best product rarely wins; the best-positioned one does. Their last company went from $0 to $8M ARR in three years on a category they had to name themselves. [Name] now helps founders make their work sound as good as it actually is. (Off the clock: still failing to finish the same short story since 2014.)*

Same person. Same facts, mostly. But the second version has a point of view, specific proof, and a human texture — it's *memorable*, and it makes the reader want the conversation. The first version was a fog of credentials; the second is an argument for the person.

### The reusable founder-bio skeleton

Run your own bio against this:

1. **The focus line:** the one problem, idea, or throughline you're known for. (Not a label — a focus.)
2. **The earned-it line:** specific, checkable experience — numbers, named outcomes, the real throughline. (Not "proven track record.")
3. **The point of view:** the contrarian-but-defensible thing you believe about your work. (The part no one can copy.)
4. **The now line:** what you do today and for whom, specifically.
5. **The human line (optional):** one *specific* personal detail, or nothing. Generic humanizing is just more fog.

If your bio is all labels and adjectives, it's doing nothing — and it's the paragraph the most important strangers read first.
