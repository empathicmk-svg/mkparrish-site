Most homepages are organized by what the company wants to say. Strong homepages are organized by the order a stranger needs to be answered. Those are different sequences, and the gap between them is where conversion leaks. A visitor arrives with a stack of questions in a specific order — *what is this, is it for me, why you, do I believe you, what now* — and if your page answers them out of order, or skips one, the reader stalls and leaves. This paid framework is the hierarchy: the exact sequence a homepage should follow, section by section, with what each one must accomplish. Hold your own page against it and the missing or out-of-order section is usually your conversion problem.

The core idea is that a homepage is a *conversation* with a predictable structure. The stranger's questions arrive in a fixed sequence, each one gating the next — they won't care *why you* until they know *what is this,* and won't *believe you* until you've given them a reason to care. Answer in their order and the page flows. Answer in yours and it stalls.

> A homepage isn't a brochure to be arranged by topic. It's a sequence of answers to a stranger's questions, asked in a fixed order. Get the order wrong and it doesn't matter how good each section is — you're answering question four while the reader is still stuck on question one.

There are six questions, in this order: **What is this? · Is it for me? · What will it do for me? · Why you, not them? · Can I believe you? · What do I do now?** Every section of a high-converting homepage maps to one of these. Let's build it.

## The Hierarchy

### 1. The hero → answers "What is this, and is it for me?"

The top of the page does exactly one job (see "The Comprehensive Homepage Is a Coward's Homepage"): make the right person understand what this is and feel it's for them, fast. Headline states what it is in plain, specific language — not clever, not category mush. Subhead names the mechanism or the audience so the right reader self-identifies. One primary CTA. **Fails if:** the headline is abstract ("The modern platform for..."), or it's trying to do all six jobs at once. **Test:** a stranger reads only the hero and can say, in their words, what you do and whether it's for them.

### 2. The problem → answers "Do you understand my situation?"

Before you talk about yourself, prove you understand *them.* Name the specific problem or tension the reader lives with, in language they'd recognize as their own. This is where trust starts — a reader who feels *seen* will keep reading. **Fails if:** you jump straight to features without establishing the problem, or you describe the problem in your jargon instead of their experience. **Test:** the reader thinks "yes, that's exactly my situation" before you've claimed anything about your solution.

### 3. The promise → answers "What will this actually do for me?"

Now the transformation — the specific, concrete outcome, not the vague benefit. Not "grow your business" but the actual before/after. This is the payoff to the problem you just named. **Fails if:** the promise is generic ("drive results," "unlock growth") or floats with no specificity. **Test:** the reader can picture the concrete after-state, not just a feeling.

### 4. The differentiation → answers "Why you and not the alternative?"

Only *now,* once they care, does "why you" land. Make the claim only you can make — the defensible position (see "The One Sentence Only You Can Write"). What do you do that the alternative can't? Why is your approach different, and better for this reader? **Fails if:** your differentiation is a feature list anyone could claim, or you never actually distinguish yourself from the obvious alternative. **Test:** a competitor couldn't paste this section onto their own site.

### 5. The proof → answers "Can I believe any of this?"

Everything above is a claim; this section backs it. Real testimonials with names and specifics (not "great to work with!"), real numbers, real logos, a real case. Proof has to *match* the claims above it — if you promised doubled demos, the proof should show a demo number. **Fails if:** proof is anonymous ("industry leaders"), vague ("amazing results"), or disconnected from the specific promises you made. **Test:** a skeptic reading only this section would extend you credit.

### 6. The CTA → answers "What do I do now?"

One clear, specific next step, framed for where the reader is in their decision. Match the commitment level to the trust you've earned — "see a sample" for the unsure, "book a call" for the warm. Remove competing actions. **Fails if:** there are multiple co-equal CTAs, the action is vague ("get started" → started how?), or the ask is bigger than the trust built. **Test:** there is exactly one obvious thing to do, and it feels proportional.

### How to run it on your page

Open your homepage and label each existing section with which of the six questions it answers. Three things show up immediately:

1. **Missing questions.** A question with no section is a gap the reader falls through. (Most commonly missing: #2, the problem — companies skip straight from "what is this" to "why you," and lose the reader who didn't feel understood first.)
2. **Out-of-order questions.** Differentiation (#4) before the problem (#2) is the classic mistake — you're bragging before you've shown you understand them. Reorder to the reader's sequence, not yours.
3. **Doubled questions.** Three sections all answering "why you" and none answering "can I believe you" means you're repeating yourself in one register while leaving another unanswered. Balance the page across all six.

The reorder alone — no new copy, just sequencing the page to the stranger's questions instead of the company's topics — routinely lifts conversion, because for the first time the page answers people in the order they're actually asking. Most homepages have decent raw material in the wrong arrangement. The hierarchy is the arrangement.
