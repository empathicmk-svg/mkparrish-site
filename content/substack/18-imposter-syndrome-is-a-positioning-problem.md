The standard advice for imposter syndrome is to manage the feeling — affirmations, reframes, "everyone feels this way," fake it till you make it. That advice treats the doubt as a psychological bug to be soothed. And sometimes it is. But in my work with founders and executives, I've come to believe that a large share of what gets called imposter syndrome is not a confidence problem at all. It's a *positioning* problem, misdiagnosed as a feeling. You don't feel like a fraud because you're inadequate. You feel like a fraud because you've never clearly defined what you actually claim to be — so there's nothing solid to stand on, and standing on vagueness feels exactly like standing on nothing.

Here's the mechanism. Imposter syndrome thrives on ambiguity. When your self-definition is fuzzy — "I'm kind of a consultant, I help with a few things, I'm still figuring out my niche" — there's no firm ground to defend, so any challenge, internal or external, sends you sliding. You can't feel like a legitimate *anything* because you haven't named the thing. The vagueness that feels like humility is actually the source of the dread. A blurry claim can be attacked from every direction, because it commits to none, and the part of you keeping watch for fraud has infinite surface area to worry about.

> A vague self-definition is impossible to feel confident in, because there's nothing specific to be confident *about*. Sharpen the claim and the doubt has less to grip. Imposter syndrome is often just the feeling of standing on ground you never bothered to define.

Watch what happens when the definition sharpens. The founder who moves from "I help businesses with marketing" to "I rewrite positioning for technical founders who can't make their work sound as good as it is" usually reports the same surprising thing: the imposter feeling drops. Not because they got more qualified overnight — they didn't — but because now there's a specific, bounded claim they can actually evaluate themselves against. "Am I good at this *specific* thing for these *specific* people?" is an answerable question, and the answer is usually yes. "Am I a legitimate *whatever-I-vaguely-am*?" is unanswerable, and the unanswerable question is the one that eats you.

## Sharpening the Claim You Stand On

If some of your imposter syndrome is a clarity problem, then some of the fix isn't therapy — it's positioning. You define the ground, and then you can finally feel solid on it.

1. **Name the specific claim.** Write down, in one concrete sentence, what you actually do and for whom — narrow enough that you could plausibly be the best at it. "Marketing" is too big to feel legitimate in; nobody is qualified to be "good at marketing." "Repositioning homepages for B2B founders" is small enough to own, and ownable things feel less fraudulent.
2. **Match the claim to your real evidence.** Imposter syndrome often spikes because your stated identity is bigger than your proof — you're claiming "expert in everything" and can only back a slice. The fix isn't to shrink yourself; it's to make the claim *precise* enough that your actual experience fully covers it. Confidence is what it feels like when your claim and your evidence are the same size.
3. **Let the boundary protect you.** A clear position includes what you *don't* do, and that boundary is load-bearing for confidence. "I don't do paid ads — I do the words the ads point to" means the gap in your ad knowledge is no longer a fraud you're hiding. It's a line you drew on purpose. You can't be an imposter at something you openly don't claim.
4. **Update the claim as you grow, deliberately.** As you expand, re-sharpen — don't let the definition blur back out into "I do a lot of things now." Every time the claim goes vague, the doubt comes back, because vagueness is the soil it grows in. Keep the definition specific and current, and you keep the ground under you firm.

I want to be careful here, because this isn't the whole story. Some imposter syndrome is real psychological weather that has nothing to do with positioning, and it deserves real care — sometimes from a professional, not a copywriter. I'm not claiming a tagline cures self-doubt. I'm claiming that a meaningful chunk of the "I feel like a fraud" experience among capable people is actually "I've never defined what I'm claiming to be," and that part *is* fixable with clarity — and it's the part that responds to positioning when affirmations don't.

So before you assume the problem is your worth, check whether the problem is your *definition.* Have you actually named, specifically, what you do and who you're for? Or have you been standing on a fog of "I sort of help with various things," wondering why it feels like you might fall through? Name the ground. Make it specific and true. You may find the fraud you were bracing against was never you — it was the blur you were standing on.
