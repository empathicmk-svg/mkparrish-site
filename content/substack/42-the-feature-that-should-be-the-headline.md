Somewhere on your website, usually halfway down, in a bullet list, in a font two sizes too small, is the single most compelling thing about what you do. The thing that, if a prospect understood it, would make them stop comparison-shopping. And you've buried it — because to you, it's just one feature among many, an item on the list, the thing you've said so many times it stopped feeling special. The most common positioning failure I see isn't a missing strength. It's a *hidden* one: the best card in the deck, played face-down in row four of the features grid.

This happens for a predictable reason. When you're close to your own product, everything feels equally important, because you know the value of every piece. So you present them democratically — a tidy grid of features, each given equal weight, the genuinely remarkable one sitting politely next to the table-stakes ones. But the reader doesn't have your context. They can't tell which feature is the showstopper and which is the checkbox, because you've flattened them all into the same list. The one thing that should make them gasp is wearing the same outfit as the thing every competitor also has.

> Founders don't usually lack a differentiator. They bury it — treating the one remarkable thing as just another bullet, because they've said it so often it stopped sounding remarkable to them. The reader is hearing it for the first time. Lead with it.

The tell is when you describe your product to a friend — a real person, casually — and you find yourself saying "the cool part is..." or "what's wild is..." or "people don't believe this, but..." Whatever follows those phrases is your headline, and it's almost never what's actually at the top of your homepage. The casual, excited, slightly-can't-help-myself description is your instinct correctly identifying the showstopper. Then you sit down to write "professionally" and bury it under "robust, scalable, end-to-end." Your gut knew the headline. Your professional voice hid it.

## Finding the Line Worth Leading With

Surfacing your buried lede is part archaeology, part nerve. A few ways to dig it out:

1. **Listen for "the cool part is..."** Describe what you do, out loud, to someone who doesn't know it, and notice where you get animated — where you lean in, where you say "and here's the thing." That spontaneous emphasis is your real headline trying to surface. Transcribe the excited version; the lede is in there.
2. **Find what makes people say "wait, really?"** The best headline is often the claim that surprises — the thing prospects don't quite believe until you prove it. Surprise earns attention; the expected earns a scroll. If one of your features reliably makes people double-take in a demo, that's the one to lead with, not bury.
3. **Ask which feature, removed, would lose the most deals.** Mentally delete each feature and ask which absence would most hurt your win rate. The one that's load-bearing for the *decision* is your differentiator — and it deserves the headline, not equal billing with the checkboxes.
4. **Demote the table stakes ruthlessly.** The features every competitor also has aren't selling points; they're the price of entry. They can live further down, briefly, for the reader who checks. Giving them top billing wastes the position on things that differentiate you from no one.

There's a discipline here that feels risky and isn't: *leading with one thing means subordinating the others*, and that subordination is exactly what makes the one thing land. A page where everything is emphasized has no emphasis (see "The Comprehensive Homepage"). When you pull your strongest feature up to the headline and let the rest support it, you're not hiding your other strengths — you're giving the reader a way in, a single sharp impression they'll actually remember. The supporting features are more persuasive *because* they're supporting a clear lead instead of competing with it.

The nerve part is real, though. Leading with your boldest feature means betting the page on it — committing, in the most visible spot, to the claim you most want to be known for. That's exposing. It's easier to hide behind a balanced grid where no single claim has to carry the weight. But balanced grids don't get remembered, and they don't get chosen. The page that bets on its best thing is the page that has a chance of landing.

So go find the line you bury. Describe your product to a friend, listen for "the cool part is," and notice how different that is from your current headline. Then have the nerve to put the cool part where the headline goes. You were never missing a differentiator. You were just too close to it to see it was the whole show.
