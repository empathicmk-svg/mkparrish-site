I can usually tell how a business is doing within the first two minutes of meeting the founder, and the tell is simple: I ask what they do, and I time the answer. The founders whose companies are working give me a sentence. Maybe two. The founders who are struggling give me four minutes — a winding, caveated, "well, it depends, we started doing X but now it's more like Y, and we also serve Z, it's hard to explain" answer that never lands anywhere. The length of the answer is inversely proportional to the health of the positioning. Long answer, lost company.

This isn't because concise founders are better talkers. It's because concision is downstream of clarity, and clarity is downstream of decisions. The founder who can answer in a sentence isn't a better communicator — they've simply made the choices that *let* the sentence exist. They've decided who they're for, what they do, and what they're not. The four-minute founder hasn't made those decisions yet, so there's no sentence to say, and they fill the void with everything, hoping the listener assembles a point out of the pile. The listener never does.

> The inability to say what you do in one sentence is not a communication problem you can fix with better words. It's a decision problem wearing a communication problem's clothes. You can't compress what you haven't yet chosen.

The cruel part is that founders experience this as a *writing* deficiency. They think: I just need a better elevator pitch, a sharper one-liner, the right copywriter to wordsmith it. So they hire for the words. And the words don't fix it, because the words were never the problem — the absence of underlying decisions was. You cannot wordsmith your way to clarity you haven't earned through choice. I've sat with founders polishing a one-liner for an hour, and the truth is always the same: the line won't come because the company hasn't decided what it is. The blank space where the sentence should be is information.

## From Four Minutes to One Sentence

Getting to the sentence is not a writing exercise. It's a deciding exercise, and the writing is just the last five minutes of it. Here's the path I walk founders down:

1. **Notice where the answer branches.** Record yourself answering "what do you do?" and listen for every "and," "but," "it depends," and "we also." Each branch is a decision you haven't made — a place where you're trying to keep two options open instead of choosing one. The branches are the work. Each one is a fork you've been refusing to take.
2. **Cut to the one you'd keep if forced.** For each branch, ask: if I could only keep one side of this, which? Not which is biggest or most profitable today — which is most *you*, most defensible, most the thing you'd want to be known for. Choosing isn't losing the other branches. It's letting one of them finally become legible.
3. **Find the verb.** Vague positioning hides in nouns ("we're a platform for...," "we're a solution that..."). Clear positioning lives in verbs — the specific thing you *do to* or *do for* someone. "We help companies with their marketing" is a noun-pile. "We rewrite the homepage that's leaking your conversion" is a verb doing work. Find the verb and the sentence starts to exist.
4. **Say it, then shut up.** Once you have the sentence, the discipline is to stop adding to it. Every clause you append to "protect" it — every "and we also," every reassuring caveat — dilutes it back toward the four-minute swamp. A clear sentence is a complete thought. Let it be complete.

There's a reason this matters beyond the awkwardness of networking events. Every single person in your business has to answer "what do they do?" on your behalf — your salespeople, your employees, your happy customers telling a friend. If *you* can't say it in a sentence, none of them can, and the version that spreads is the garbled four-minute one, told secondhand, getting worse with each retelling. Your clarity is the ceiling on everyone else's. A company can only be described as well as its founder can describe it.

So time yourself. Ask someone to ask you what you do, and watch the clock. If the answer runs long, resist the urge to find better words. Go find the decisions you've been avoiding — the branches you won't cut, the fork you keep straddling. The sentence is waiting on the other side of a choice. It always was.
