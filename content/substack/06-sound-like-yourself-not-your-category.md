Open five homepages in the same category and read them back to back. Do it with project management tools, or fractional CFOs, or skincare, or AI note-takers — it does not matter which. By the third one you will start to feel a specific kind of vertigo, because they are all saying the same thing in the same shape. *Built for modern teams. Effortless. Finally, a [thing] that just works. Spend less time on X so you can focus on what matters.* Five different companies, one voice, and that voice belongs to none of them. It belongs to the category.

This is not laziness, exactly. It is gravity. Every category develops a dialect — a set of words, claims, and sentence rhythms that signal "I am a legitimate player here." When you are new and unsure, speaking the dialect feels safe. It is how you prove you belong in the room. So you absorb it, usually without noticing, by reading your competitors before you write your own copy. And the moment you do that, you have handed your voice to the average of everyone who came before you. You sound like the category because you learned to speak from the category.

> Category language is camouflage. It makes you safe by making you invisible. The whole point of camouflage is that nobody can pick you out of the background — which is a strange goal for a company that needs to be chosen.

The trap is that the dialect *feels* like professionalism. Founders read their own category-fluent copy and think, this sounds like a real company. And it does. It sounds exactly like a real company — like all of them, indistinguishably, in a way that gives a buyer no reason to pick you over the next tab. You have optimized for sounding legitimate and accidentally optimized against being memorable, and in a crowded market memorable is the only thing that survives the comparison.

## How to Hear Your Own Voice Again

Voice is not a flourish you add at the end. It is what is left when you stop borrowing. Getting it back is less about generating something new and more about noticing what you've imported and refusing it. A few ways I do this with clients:

- **Read your draft and flag every phrase a competitor could also use.** Highlight them. The highlighted parts are the category talking. The un-highlighted parts — usually the specific, slightly awkward, true bits — are you. Cut toward the un-highlighted.
- **Find the sentence you almost deleted for being too blunt.** It is almost always the best sentence you wrote. The category dialect has no room for blunt; bluntness is a fingerprint. Keep it.
- **Replace one abstraction with the specific thing it's hiding.** "Streamline your workflow" is category. "Stop re-typing the same address into four systems" is you. Specificity is the fastest exit from the dialect, because the category only ever speaks in abstractions — abstractions are what let everyone claim the same ground.
- **Write the way you'd explain it to a smart friend over coffee, then clean up the grammar but not the voice.** The coffee version is almost never category-fluent. It has opinions and asides and the occasional curse. That's the raw material. Most "professionalizing" is just sanding the voice off.

Notice that none of these are about adding personality. You do not need to bolt on quirk or manufacture a "brand voice" out of adjectives. The voice is already there, in how you actually talk about the work when no one is grading you. The job is subtraction — removing the layer of borrowed category language sitting on top of it. Underneath the camouflage there is a real person who knows exactly why this thing is different. Let them talk.

One caution, because the overcorrection is real: sounding like yourself is not the same as being weird for its own sake. The goal is not maximum quirk. It is accuracy. The most distinctive voice in any category is usually just the most *precise* one — the company willing to say the specific, slightly uncomfortable, true thing while everyone else hides in the comfortable abstraction. You don't have to be loud to be unmistakable. You have to be exact.

Go read your homepage one more time, and ask the only question that matters: if I covered the logo, would anyone who knows this category be able to tell it was us? If the honest answer is no, you don't have a copy problem. You have a camouflage problem. Take it off.
