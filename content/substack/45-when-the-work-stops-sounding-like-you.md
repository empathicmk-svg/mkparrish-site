There's a particular grief that successful people don't talk about: the moment you read something you wrote and realize it doesn't sound like you anymore. The work is competent. It's professional. It's getting results. And it's also become a kind of smooth, voiceless version of itself — sanded down, de-risked, agreeable. Somewhere along the way, in the process of getting good and getting hired and getting it right, the specific, slightly strange, unmistakably-*you* voice you started with quietly left the building. And the strangest part is that nothing went wrong. The drift happened *because* things went right.

Here's the mechanism, because it's worth understanding so you can catch it. Every success teaches you a lesson, and you internalize it. A bold line got criticized once, so you soften the next one. A safe piece performed fine, so you make the next one a little safer. A client preferred the smoother version, so you smooth by default. Each adjustment is individually reasonable — you're learning, optimizing, responding to feedback. But they accumulate in one direction: away from risk, away from edge, away from the specific choices that made your voice yours. You don't decide to sound generic. You get *trained* into it, one reasonable concession at a time.

> Nobody chooses to lose their voice. They optimize their way out of it — sanding off one sharp edge at a time because each edge, individually, felt risky, until the thing that's left is smooth, successful, and sounds like no one.

The cruel irony is that the voice you're sanding off is usually the thing that made you successful in the first place. The early work that got attention had edges — it took positions, it sounded like a particular person, it was willing to be disagreed with. That *specificity* was the draw. And then, in the name of getting better, you trade the specificity for polish, not realizing you're trading away the exact asset that built the thing. You optimize the voice right out of the work and wonder why the work, though more competent than ever, has stopped landing the way it used to.

## Finding Your Way Back

Recovering a drifted voice isn't about burning down the competence you've built. It's about *adding the edges back* — reintroducing the specificity that the smoothing removed. A few ways back:

1. **Read your old work — the early stuff.** Before the optimization, before the polish. Notice what's there that's missing now: the bolder claims, the weirder metaphors, the willingness to be specific and disagreeable. That early voice isn't gone; it's buried under accumulated caution. Reading it reminds you what you sound like when you're not managing risk.
2. **Find the line you'd cut, and keep it.** When you draft now, notice the sentence your instinct says is "too much" — too blunt, too personal, too opinionated. That instinct is the trained caution talking. The line it wants to cut is often the most alive thing you wrote. Keep one. Then another. Re-teach yourself that the edge is allowed.
3. **Write something you won't show anyone.** The drift is partly a function of audience — you've been writing *for* approval so long that the approval-seeking happens automatically. Write a piece with no audience, no client, no metrics, just to hear yourself again. The voice tends to come back fastest when nothing's at stake.
4. **Re-decide what you're willing to be disagreed with about.** Voice requires positions, and positions require accepting that some people will disagree. Somewhere in the drift, you started avoiding disagreement. Pick one thing you actually believe about your work that not everyone would — and say it, on purpose, in public. That single act of re-edging does more than any amount of "be more authentic."

I want to be honest about the tension, because it's real: some of the smoothing was *growth.* Not every edge you lost was worth keeping — some early-work roughness really was just inexperience, and learning to cut it was the right call. The skill is telling the difference between the edges that were *inexperience* (worth losing) and the edges that were *voice* (worth protecting). The drift is dangerous precisely because it removes both kinds indiscriminately, in the name of "getting more polished," until the voice goes out with the inexperience.

There's a deeper layer here that's about identity, not just craft. The work sounding like you and *you feeling like yourself* are connected. When your output drifts into voiceless competence, it often mirrors a quieter drift in how you're living — toward the agreeable, the de-risked, the version of yourself optimized for approval. Recovering your voice on the page is sometimes the first move in recovering it everywhere else. The pen, as it turns out, is rarely just about the pen.

So read something you wrote recently and ask the uncomfortable question: does this sound like me, or does it sound like a competent stranger who's very good at not getting criticized? If it's the stranger, you haven't lost anything permanent. The voice is still there, under the polish, waiting for you to decide the edges are worth the risk again. They are. They always were. That's what made it yours.
