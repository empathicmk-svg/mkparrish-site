The most expensive two words in professional services are "it depends." They feel like honesty. They feel like nuance, like the mark of someone experienced enough to know that real answers are complicated. And on a sales call, with rapport already built, a thoughtful "well, it depends — here's the tradeoff" can be exactly right. But on your homepage, in your copy, in the first thirty seconds a stranger spends deciding whether you're worth a conversation, "it depends" is a door closing. The buyer didn't come for nuance. They came for a reason to believe you can solve their problem, and "it depends" tells them you won't commit to one.

Qualified copy is everywhere in expert businesses, and it comes from a good place. The more you actually know, the more aware you are of exceptions, edge cases, and the conditions under which your advice would change. So you hedge, because hedging is intellectually honest. The problem is that the reader cannot tell the difference between *earned caution* and *no point of view.* Both render on the page as the same gray mush of "every situation is unique" and "results may vary," and that mush is indistinguishable from the copy of someone who simply doesn't know what they think.

> A buyer hires conviction, not caveats. The expert who says "here's what I'd do, and here's why" wins the engagement over the expert who says "it depends on many factors" — even when the second expert is right, because the second one gave the buyer nothing to hold onto.

There's a counterintuitive truth underneath this. The person most qualified to make a bold, clear claim is the actual expert — because they've earned the right to it through experience, and they can defend it. Yet experts are the ones most likely to hedge, precisely *because* they see the complexity. Meanwhile the loudest, clearest, most confident voices in a lot of categories belong to people who know the least, because ignorance has no edge cases to worry about. So expertise and confidence end up inversely correlated in the copy, and the buyer — who reads confidence as competence — picks the confident amateur over the hedging expert. That's the real cost of "it depends." It hands your market to people who know less than you.

## Saying the Clear Thing Anyway

The fix is not to pretend the complexity doesn't exist. It's to lead with a position and *then* hold the nuance, instead of leading with the nuance and never arriving at a position. The order is the entire difference.

- **State your actual answer first, in one sentence.** Whatever you'd tell a client after the "it depends" — the recommendation you'd actually make — that's the line. Lead with it. "Most founders should rewrite the homepage before touching ads." Then qualify, if you must. The claim goes first because the claim is what the reader can use.
- **Make the caveat a footnote, not the headline.** Nuance belongs in the second paragraph, the fine print, the deeper page — places a committed reader goes after the claim has already hooked them. It does not belong in the sentence that's supposed to make them care.
- **Replace "it depends" with "here's how I'd decide."** If the answer genuinely varies, don't hide behind the variation — expose the decision rule. "If you're under $1M in revenue, do X; over that, do Y." That's still a clear, confident, usable point of view. It just happens to be a conditional one. The reader gets a tool instead of a shrug.
- **Trust that conviction with a track record reads as wisdom, not arrogance.** Founders hedge because they're afraid a bold claim sounds cocky. It doesn't — not when it's specific and defensible. It sounds like someone who has done this enough times to know. That's the thing the buyer is shopping for.

The deeper issue is that "it depends" is often not caution at all. It's avoidance. Committing to a clear position is exposing — someone could disagree, someone could hold you to it. The hedge protects you from that exposure, and it protects you right out of the consideration set. Every time you refuse to plant a flag, you make it a little easier for the buyer to choose the person who did.

You know more than the confident amateurs winning the engagements you should be winning. The only thing standing between you and those engagements is a willingness to say the clear thing out loud. Say it. The nuance can wait for the second paragraph — where a buyer who's already convinced will actually read it.
