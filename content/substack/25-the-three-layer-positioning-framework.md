Most positioning advice fails because it hands you a destination without a route. "Find your unique value proposition." Great — *how?* The reason founders stay stuck in generic positioning isn't that they don't know they should be specific. It's that "be specific" isn't a process. So this is the actual process — the three-layer framework I run with clients, in order, with the questions that force the decisions. It's the paid version of every free essay I've written about positioning, because the free essays tell you *what's* true and this tells you exactly *how* to get there on your own business.

The framework has three layers because positioning fails at three distinct altitudes, and most people only work on one. They obsess over the surface (the words, the tagline) while the foundation (who it's for, what it's against) sits undecided. You have to build from the bottom up: **Audience → Argument → Articulation.** Skip a layer and the ones above it collapse, which is why so much "rebranding" is just rewording — it's surface work poured over a foundation nobody set.

> Positioning is not one decision. It's three, stacked, in a fixed order: who you're for, what you're claiming against the conventional wisdom, and how you say it. Work them bottom-up. Most brands are stuck because they keep editing Layer 3 to fix a problem that lives in Layer 1.

Here's the whole shape before we go deep. **Layer 1, Audience:** the specific person, defined narrowly enough to exclude. **Layer 2, Argument:** the claim you're making and the conventional wisdom you're making it against. **Layer 3, Articulation:** the words — the sentence, the tagline, the homepage — which should fall out almost effortlessly once 1 and 2 are decided. If Layer 3 is hard, you haven't finished 1 and 2.

## Layer 1 — Audience: Who, narrowly enough to exclude

You cannot position for "everyone," and you cannot position for a demographic. You position for a *specific person in a specific situation.* This layer is done when you can describe one human so precisely that the wrong prospects feel it's not for them.

**The questions, in order:**

1. **Who is the single best client you've ever had** — the one you'd clone? Not the biggest. The best-fit: the one where the work was great and the result was clear. Name them.
2. **What was true about their *situation*** at the moment they came to you? Not their industry — their *circumstance.* ("Just raised a seed round and realized the deck that worked for investors doesn't work for customers." That's a situation. "B2B SaaS" is not.)
3. **What did they believe, or fear, or want** that made them ready for you specifically? The internal state, in their words.
4. **Who is this explicitly *not* for?** Name the prospects you should turn away. If your answer is "anyone could benefit," you haven't done Layer 1 — you've avoided it.

**Output of Layer 1:** one sentence — *"For [specific person] who is [specific situation] and believes/fears/wants [internal state]."* Worked example: *"For the technical founder who just hit product-market fit and realizes no one can tell what their product does from the homepage."*

## Layer 2 — Argument: What you claim, and what you claim it against

Positioning is an argument, and an argument needs an opponent. This layer is where you stop describing yourself and start *disagreeing with something* — because a claim with no opponent is just a feature, and features don't position.

**The questions, in order:**

1. **What's the conventional wisdom in your category** that your best clients have come to distrust? The thing "everyone says" that's actually wrong, or incomplete, or quietly failing people. (e.g., "more content is better," "you need a rebrand," "AI can write your copy now.")
2. **What do you believe instead** — the contrarian-but-defensible position you'd stake your reputation on? State it as a claim someone could disagree with. If no one could disagree, it's not an argument, it's a platitude.
3. **What's the one thing you do** that proves the claim — the method, the result, the way of working that a generic competitor can't credibly copy?
4. **What's the cost of your position** — who does it turn off, what does it commit you to? (A position with no cost isn't a position. The cost is the proof it means something.)

**Output of Layer 2:** *"Everyone in our category believes [conventional wisdom]. We believe [contrarian claim], and we prove it by [the one thing]."* Worked example: *"Everyone says founders need more content. We believe most founders need *less, sharper* content — one homepage that actually says what they do beats fifty blog posts — and we prove it by rewriting the one page that's leaking their conversion."*

## Layer 3 — Articulation: The words, which should now be easy

If Layers 1 and 2 are genuinely decided, this layer is fast — that's the test. The sentence falls out of the decisions. If you're agonizing here, go back; the agony means a decision above is still open.

**The questions, in order:**

1. **What's the plainest possible sentence** that combines your audience (L1) and your argument (L2)? Plain first. Clever later, maybe never.
2. **Does it pass the competitor test?** Could a rival run it unchanged? If yes, it's still category language — push it back toward your specific L2 claim.
3. **Does it pass the wince test?** Does it make you slightly nervous because it's specific and excludes someone? If it's perfectly comfortable, it's probably too safe to position anything.
4. **Can you defend it?** If a skeptic said "prove it," do you have the L2 proof ready? If not, you're claiming more than you've decided.

**Output of Layer 3:** the homepage headline, the one-liner, the tagline — all of it, drawn from the same decided foundation so they reinforce instead of contradict each other. Worked example headline: *"Your product is better than your homepage makes it sound. Let's fix the homepage."* (Audience: the PMF technical founder. Argument: the page, not more content. Articulation: plain, specific, winces a little, defensible.)

### How to run this on yourself this week

Block ninety minutes. Do the layers *in order* and resist jumping to Layer 3 — the temptation to start wordsmithing is the exact thing that keeps people stuck. Write the output sentence for each layer before moving up. When you hit a question you can't answer, you've found your real positioning problem; that unanswered question is the work, not a thing to skip past. Most founders discover their whole "branding problem" was actually one undecided question in Layer 1 or 2 — and once it's decided, the words they fought for months arrive in an afternoon.
