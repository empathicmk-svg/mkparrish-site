A founder came to me last month with a tagline problem. He had been through four agencies and roughly forty options. He had a spreadsheet. He had favorites, and his cofounder had different favorites, and the two of them had not agreed on a single line in six weeks. He wanted me to break the tie.

I read all forty. Then I read his homepage, his sales deck, and the last three things he'd posted on LinkedIn. And I told him the thing he did not want to hear, which is that none of the forty taglines were the problem, because the company did not yet know what it was. You cannot write the five words on the marquee until you know what the building contains. He had been polishing the sign on a store with no inventory decision made.

This is the most common expensive mistake I see, and it is expensive precisely because it feels like progress. Wordsmithing the tagline is satisfying. It is concrete, it is bounded, it produces visible options you can vote on. It feels like the kind of decisive, founder-y work that moves a brand forward. Meanwhile the actual question — who is this for, what do they currently believe, and what do we need them to believe instead — sits untouched, because that question is uncomfortable and has no clean spreadsheet.

> A tagline is a compression. It can only compress something that already exists. If the thing underneath is vague, the tagline will be vague no matter how clever the words are — and if the thing underneath is sharp, almost any honest sentence will work.

Here is the test I gave him, and the one I'll give you. Take your current tagline and hand it to someone in your category — a competitor, a peer, anyone selling roughly what you sell. Could they put it on their homepage without changing a word? If the answer is yes, the tagline is not failing. The positioning is. You have written something true about the category instead of something true about you, and the reason you did that is almost always that you have not yet decided what is true about you specifically. The generic tagline is a symptom. The disease is upstream.

## What the Tagline Is Actually Sitting On

A tagline is the last five percent of a positioning decision. The other ninety-five percent is the part nobody wants to do, because it requires saying no to things, and saying no to things in public feels like leaving money on the table. It is not. It is the only way the five words ever land.

Underneath every tagline that works, four decisions have already been made — and made specifically, not aspirationally:

1. **Who it is for, stated narrowly enough to exclude someone.** "For ambitious teams" excludes no one and therefore positions nothing. "For the second-time founder who got burned by their first agency" excludes most of the market on purpose, which is exactly why the right person feels it in their chest.
2. **What they currently believe that is wrong.** Positioning is an argument, and an argument needs an opponent. If you cannot name the conventional wisdom you're correcting, you have a description, not a position.
3. **The one thing you do that the alternative cannot.** Not the ten things you do well. The one thing that, if a prospect understood it, would make the comparison shopping stop.
4. **What you refuse to be.** The boundary is the brand. Most strong positioning is as defined by what it walks away from as by what it claims.

Make those four decisions and the tagline writes itself in an afternoon. Skip them and you will spend six weeks in a spreadsheet, because you are asking five words to carry a load that five words were never meant to carry. No sentence is load-bearing enough to hold up a company that hasn't decided what it is.

The founder and I never picked from his forty options. We threw the spreadsheet out. We spent two sessions on the four decisions above, and at the end of the second one he said a sentence out loud, almost as an aside, describing who he was actually for and what he was actually correcting. That sentence — lightly edited, eight words — became the tagline. It had not been on the list of forty, because the list of forty had been generated before the company knew the answer. You can't shortlist a sentence you don't yet have the conviction to write.

So if you are stuck on your tagline, I'd gently suggest you are not stuck on your tagline. Put the options away. Answer the four questions, specifically and uncomfortably, and let the line fall out of the answers. The words were never the work. The decision was.
