## The Boy Who Talked to the Stars

In a kingdom at the edge of the sea lived a boy named Theo, who did not do things the way the other children did.

When the market went loud and clanging, Theo pressed his hands over his ears until the noise stopped climbing up his skin. And off at the edge, he lined up his stones — smallest to largest, dark to light, in a row as straight as a ruler.

Off in his own world, the townspeople said. And they were right about one thing. Theo *did* have a world. It was only that nobody had ever thought to ask him what was in it.

But every night, when the town went dark and the noise went quiet, Theo climbed the hill behind his house and looked up. And the stars — the stars *talked* to him. Not in words. Theo did not always trust words; they were slippery things that came too fast. The stars spoke in patterns. In the slow, sure turning of the sky.

He knew them all. Where each one rose. Where it set. Which ones traveled together, and which stood alone. The sky never got too loud. It never asked him to hurry. And up there, Theo felt the thing he almost never felt in town. He felt like he *fit.*

Then, one autumn, a trouble came. The fishing boats went out and did not come home. A fog rolled in off the sea — thick and gray and sudden — and the boats, which steered by the shore, lost the shore, and drifted, frightened, in the white.

The whole town gathered, and the grown-ups argued. Bigger lanterns! Louder bells! Longer ropes! But none of it works in a fog you cannot see or hear through. The square got louder and louder — and at the edge, hands over his ears, Theo was listening. Theo was always listening, even when it looked like he was somewhere else.

And when a small gap opened in the noise, Theo stood up. His heart pounded. The words wanted to stick. But this was too important. So he pushed them out, one at a time, the way he pushed his stones into a line.

"The stars," said Theo. And the whole square turned to look at him.

"The fog is low. It sits on the water. But it is thin at the top. Look straight up, and the stars come through. And the stars don't move like the land moves. They come back to the same place every night, exactly. When you can't see the shore," said Theo, "look up. The sky will bring you home."

The oldest fisherman, a man with a face like a cracked map, said quietly, "The boy's right. Sailors used to do it, long ago. We forgot. But nobody here remembers the stars well enough to teach it."

"I do," said Theo. And he did.

All winter, Theo stood on the hill and taught the fishermen the sky. He was patient in a way that surprised them — because the sky had taught him patience first. He never rushed. He never flustered. And for the first time, the whole town was grateful for exactly the way Theo's mind worked.

So when the fog came now, the fishermen tipped their heads back, found Theo's stars, and the sky brought every last boat home.

Theo still sat at the edge, most days, lining up his stones. That did not change. It did not need to. But now a few of the braver children came to sit beside him — not to pull him into their game, but to learn his. To go quiet. To look up.

Theo showed them the sky.

## The End — And Your Beginning

The world is full of Theos — children who line things up, know everything about one enormous thing, and cover their ears when it all gets too loud. For a long time the world told them the way their minds worked was a problem to be fixed.

The world was wrong.

Theo did not save the boats by becoming like everyone else. He saved them by being *exactly himself.* If you are a Theo, hold on to this: your mind is not broken. It is tuned to something the rest of the world got too busy to hear. One day the fog will come, and everyone will be shouting, and you — quiet, careful, looking up — will be the one who knows the way home.
